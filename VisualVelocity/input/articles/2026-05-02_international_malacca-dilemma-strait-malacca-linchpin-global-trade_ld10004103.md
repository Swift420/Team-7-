# The Malacca dilemma: The Strait of Malacca is the linchpin of global trade. Is it threatened with a scenario similar to that of the Strait of Hormuz?

*The strait near Singapore is even more important for global shipping than the entrance to the Persian Gulf. Hundreds of ships pass through it daily. A blockade would severely impact China.*

Anyone who has ever landed at Singapore airport is familiar with the sight: At any time of day or night, hundreds of ships can be seen on approach. Tankers, freighters, tugboats, fishing boats and, somewhat less frequently, cruise ships or warships. They all pass through the Strait of Malacca.

The Strait of Malacca and the connecting Strait of Singapore are the linchpin of global trade. They link the Indian Ocean with the Pacific Ocean. To the north, India controls the western approach, while further east in the South China Sea, China is the dominant power. Most shipping traffic between East Asia and the Indian subcontinent, the Middle East, Africa and Europe passes through here. The strait is 800 kilometers long, but at its narrowest point near Singapore, it is only three kilometers wide.

## A US leverage against China

The possibility of a blockade of the Strait of Malacca has long concerned him, says the head of security for the Asia-Pacific region at a global shipping company. Because making political statements is sensitive for shipping companies, he wishes to remain anonymous.

According to the industry insider, the main scenario pursued so far has been this: if China imposes a blockade on Taiwan or attacks the island, the US will create a counter-blockade in the Strait of Malacca for tankers heading to China. This would cut off the People's Republic from supply sources in the Middle East.

The current closure of the Strait of Hormuz in the Persian Gulf severely impacts the world's oil supply. A closure of the Strait of Malacca would further disrupt freight traffic. However, alternative routes exist, such as the Sunda Strait near Jakarta or the Lombok Strait further east near the island of Bali.

While it would be possible to reroute shipping traffic via these alternative routes instead of Singapore, says the shipping company representative, this would entail considerable disadvantages. The Sunda Route extends the journey between East Asia and Europe by at least 1,000 kilometers. However, this strait is not deep enough for the largest container ships, which can carry up to 24,000 containers and have a draft of up to 17 meters.

Ships this large would have to navigate the deeper Strait of Lombok. The largest oil tankers, so-called «ultra-large crude carriers,» which transport more than 3 million barrels of oil, already use this route. The problem: The journey becomes 2,000 kilometers longer. Compared to the route through the Strait of Malacca, the travel time increases by three days.

Furthermore, the ports along the alternative routes do not offer the same facilities for transshipment, refueling or repairs as the major ports along the Strait of Malacca, says Ja-Ian Chong, professor of political science at the National University of Singapore: «It is these services that make Singapore so important today.»

## Which side will Singapore take in the event of a conflict?

The fact that alternatives to the Strait of Malacca exist massively increases the effort required for a blockade. To prevent oil from the Middle East from reaching China, the Americans would also have to intercept tankers on routes in Indonesia. «The U.S. Navy would probably be capable of doing so, but it would have to divert resources from other regions – where they would then be lacking,» says Collin Koh, a maritime security expert at the S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies in Singapore.

The Americans maintain a permanent military presence in Singapore. However, this is primarily logistical. While no warships are stationed at this strategic location, they frequently call at the city. Nevertheless, from a Chinese perspective, the close ties between Singapore and the U.S. are a cause for concern.

«Beijing fears that Singapore would side with the U.S. in a crisis,» says political scientist Chong, «but whether that would actually happen will only become clear when the crisis occurs.» Singapore has no formal obligation to support the U.S., he added.

Singapore is doing everything it can to avoid such a situation – it doesn't want to be caught in the crossfire between its security partner and its most important trading partner. That's why ships of the Chinese navy and coast guard are regularly allowed to call at the Changi naval base in Singapore.

## China wants to defuse the Malacca dilemma

Despite the significant challenge that effectively implementing a blockade would pose for the Americans, the communist leadership in Beijing is taking the scenario seriously. In 2003, the then General Secretary of the Communist Party and head of state, Hu Jintao, declared that «certain major powers» were intent on controlling the strait. He was, of course, referring to the United States.

Since then, the People's Republic has made great efforts to defuse the «Malacca Dilemma»: It has built up large reserves of oil and gas, promoted alternative energies and constructed pipelines and railway lines that bypass the Strait of Malacca. This has reduced China's dependence on supplies from the Persian Gulf.

However, the capacity is insufficient to become independent of the Strait of Malacca. Especially in freight transport, the railway cannot compete with the enormous cargo ships – it takes more than 150 trains to transport as many containers as a single large vessel.

## India and China could also close the Strait of Malacca

Another of Beijing's rivals might be tempted to disrupt its oil supplies: India. Delhi and Beijing are locked in a dispute over vast territories along their shared border in the Himalayas. If war breaks out there, India could exert pressure through a blockade. India holds a geographical trump card: the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. These stretch more than 700 kilometers north-south off the western entrance to the Strait of Malacca.

In recent years, Delhi has expanded its bases along this natural sea barrier and also stationed surveillance aircraft there. However, naval expert Koh doubts that it would be capable of sealing off the vast sea area and preventing oil tankers from sailing to China.

Finally, China itself might be tempted to partially disrupt maritime traffic between Europe and Asia. When the U.S. becomes involved in a conflict anywhere in the world, it draws on forces from distant regions. For the war against Iran, the Americans deployed amphibious landing craft and destroyers from Japan to the Middle East – through the Strait of Malacca.

This would also be the case in a war over Taiwan. For American units in the Middle East, Europe or Africa, the fastest route to Taiwan would lead through the Strait of Malacca.

The Strait of Malacca opens into the South China Sea. Ten years ago, China created artificial islands by building up several reefs there – and militarized them despite promises to the contrary. These military bases are equipped with radar for air and sea surveillance, batteries of missiles for use against ships and aircraft and serve as bases for Chinese warships and fighter jets.

This makes it impossible for the American Navy and Air Force to cross the South China Sea undetected by the Chinese. And anyone who is discovered will be fought. Moving military resources to the Taiwan region in this way would be extremely risky for the U.S.
