# There are sensible ways out of the looming sovereign debt crisis.

*In the US, in France, and perhaps soon in Germany as well, national debts are spiraling out of control. This is not fate, but a sign of a lack of responsibility and control.*

The situation is serious, but not hopeless.

Numerous Western states have repeatedly responded to increasing demands on the public sector and to crises with the same nonchalant attitude in recent decades: they spend ever more and finance this with national debt, which they then fail to reduce during prosperous times. As a result, many countries are now more heavily indebted than they were after the Second World War.

The increase in government debt relative to economic output was particularly pronounced in the USA and in the southern European countries. France clearly belongs to southern Europe in this respect. Northern European countries, on the other hand, have on average managed to keep their debt levels under control. Eastern European countries are also in a relatively sound financial position. In contrast, Central European countries have only managed a half-hearted stabilization at an excessively high level in recent years.

Germany's debt-to-GDP ratio is currently only 64 percent. However, according to forecasts by the International Monetary Fund (IMF), it is expected to rise by a full 10 percentage points over the next five years. Switzerland, consolidated across its federal levels, is at a level comparable to other northern European countries with a debt-to-GDP ratio of 37 percent – Sweden (34 percent) and Denmark (30 percent) have slightly lower levels of debt.

The high and still-increasing debt levels of many developed countries are a problem. They restrict these countries' fiscal flexibility. For the past three years, the US has been spending significantly more on interest payments on its national debt than on its military. France also pays more in interest than it spends on its armed forces. Both countries have chronically unbalanced deficits throughout the economic cycle. They pay their interest with new debt. If this continues, it will sooner or later lead to a sovereign debt crisis, like the one Greece experienced starting in 2009.

## It's not just "rich capitalists" who are affected.

Sovereign debt crises are the worst and most expensive form of debt relief. They are usually associated with forced, substantial debt write-offs. This not only puts "wealthy capitalists" in a difficult position, but typically also banks, pension funds, insurance companies, and their customers.

To protect taxpayers, central banks often step in and buy up debt. However, when government debt is monetized on a large scale, it leads to high inflation. Small savers are less able to protect themselves against this than wealthy capitalists.

It is therefore far better to balance public finances in good time. This is especially true at present. After government revenues flowed abundantly until recently, the geopolitical and geoeconomic situation has now deteriorated. The public sector must prepare for significantly higher defense spending. Consolidation is most easily achieved when it is linked to reforms that promote economic growth.

## There are good role models.

Sweden, for example, has shown that this is possible. Under the left-wing socialists, government spending rose seemingly incessantly in the 1980s and 1990s. An ever-increasing share of annual economic output was consumed by the state. The government spending ratio climbed to over 60 percent. The economy began to falter. With high taxes, more and more Swedes focused on extracting the maximum personal benefit from their expensive state.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 5f0d990f01eef2d9275d2ca603a2a5e5]

During the Scandinavian banking and financial crisis of 1990 to 1994, it became clear to everyone that things could not continue as they were. Sweden put its public finances on a more solid footing with a whole series of reforms. Old-age pensions were converted to a fully funded system, and pension entitlements were linked to life expectancy. Business-friendly and competition-oriented tax and labor market reforms stimulated growth. As a result, the ratio of government debt to economic output has halved in the past quarter-century, from 69 to 34 percent.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 68d425d4699e9424f42875706cc96c62]

Even Portugal, which had to overcome a severe banking crisis during the financial crisis, has managed to achieve sustained primary surpluses and respectable economic growth (with the exception of the pandemic) through consistent austerity measures and reforms. As a result, Portugal's debt level has fallen from 133 to 91 percent of GDP. The IMF forecasts a further reduction to 77 percent within the next five years.

## What helps

There are therefore better ways out of a sovereign debt crisis than insolvency. Moreover, the sooner and more unambiguously a government changes course and communicates this convincingly, the less painful the consequences will be. Market participants are then more willing to offer new loans at lower interest rates.

To ensure that consolidation succeeds or is not even necessary in the first place, several principles, repeatedly pointed out by economists and the IMF, can help:

In a comprehensive econometric analysis, the IMF examined the efficiency of public spending in 151 countries between 1980 and 2023. The analysis differentiated between spending on infrastructure, health, education, and public research. The study reached the remarkable conclusion that there is a 31 percent gap between the best benchmark and the average efficiency of government spending in industrialized countries. If best practices were applied everywhere, the same results could be achieved with one-third less funding.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 68d425d4699e9424f42875706caef74a]

The efficiency gap in infrastructure spending was particularly pronounced in the 1980s. Greater transparency and public tenders have improved the situation somewhat since then, but the use of public funds remains unnecessarily high. Savings are rarely made in public buildings and schools. The potential for savings is also considerable in healthcare, and the use of public funds for research appears to have even increased over time.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 68d425d4699e9424f42875706cb8f7f4]

Substantial savings potential also exists in the salaries of administrative staff. According to IMF calculations, public employees in industrialized countries earn on average 5 percent more than their counterparts in the private sector (13 percent more in emerging economies). A study by the Swiss Institute for Economic Policy found that in Switzerland, the federal government pays its employees with the same qualifications and experience 12 percent more, the cantons 4 percent more, and the municipalities 3 percent more.

## Discipline pays off

Savings could therefore be achieved by reducing wage levels and the number of employees. A reduction in bureaucracy usually achieves both: it lowers government spending and increases economic growth. Ideally, parliaments and governments should be regularly required to scrutinize all regulations to determine whether they are truly necessary and could be simplified.

A debt mountain that grows steadily relative to economic output, as currently seen in the US and major European countries, is a sign of a lack of responsibility and control. This can lead to sovereign default and harsh debt restructuring. While Greece's economy is now growing again, prosperity is still lower than before the crisis.

The Greek example should serve as a sufficient deterrent in discussions about taking on additional debt outside the regular budget. Ironically, it was the Germans who forced the Greeks to implement austerity measures back then. Unfortunately, the Portuguese and the Swedes cannot now force the Germans to follow suit.
