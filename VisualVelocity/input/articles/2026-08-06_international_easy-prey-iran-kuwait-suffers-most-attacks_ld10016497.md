# Easy Prey for Iran: Kuwait suffers most from attacks on the Gulf

*Kuwait has long been overshadowed by Qatar and Dubai. Iranian attacks on the Gulf emirate deepen the Kuwaiti crisis – and bring an old trauma back to the surface.*

«Should you hear a siren, please proceed immediately to the basement,» says the receptionist at an American chain hotel in Kuwait City. «Welcome!» Unlike Dubai or Doha, Kuwait was not a holiday destination even before the outbreak of the Iran war. But now, hardly any foreigners come to the small Gulf emirate on Iraq’s southern border. The massive hotel block in the center of the city-state currently has roughly as many guests as employees.

Since Iran and the U.S. resumed their mutual shelling, Kuwait has been the hardest hit. Iranian missiles struck, among other targets, a Kuwaiti power plant and a desalination facility. The latter, in particular, is especially concerning for the emirate, which extracts 90% of its drinking water from the sea. In total, since the war began at the end of February, Iran has launched nearly 1,500 missiles and drones at the country, which is not even half the size of Switzerland.

The Iran war hits Kuwait, like other Gulf countries, to the core. But in the city-state, the wailing of sirens and the shelling evoke particularly strong fear. «Because of the invasion, we always have this deep-seated fear that someone from outside will come and take over our country,» says architect Hamad Alfawaz in his office in one of Kuwait’s many modest glass towers.

The invasion of Iraq was over 35 years ago, but it still burdens the collective psyche of Kuwaitis. In 1990, Iraqi dictator Saddam Hussein sent tanks rolling into the small neighboring country and occupied it for a year, until the U.S. drove Saddam’s troops out in Operation «Desert Storm.» Kuwait has not recovered from the shock to this day. Just as back then, the country’s future hangs in the balance today.

## Kuwait’s long decline

![Since the war began, architect Hamad Alfawaz’s business has been struggling.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/07/29/2d7d9841-27bf-4df6-8258-93e96f683597.jpg?crop=287,402,x0,y0)
*Since the war began, architect Hamad Alfawaz’s business has been struggling.* (PD)

Kuwait, once the pearl of the Gulf, has experienced a spectacular decline. «Until the 1980s, we were what Dubai is today,» says Hamad Alfawaz. Now, the emirate even had to temporarily put diesel generators into operation to cover the high electricity consumption during the scorching summer after the missile strike on the power plant.

In the 1960s and 1970s, Kuwait was the gateway to the Gulf. It was the first country to possess a sovereign wealth fund, and the Emir financed projects around the world with immense oil wealth – including in what was then an unknown fishing town called Dubai. Nightclubs flourished in Kuwait, and global stars like Andy Warhol stopped in the city-state.

Already in the 1980s, the emirate was caught between the fronts in the Iran-Iraq war. Then, in 1990, it became prey to Saddam Hussein itself. Since then, Kuwait has tried not to attract attention, and not to arouse covetousness among its large neighbors. Since the Iraqi invasion, the emirate has primarily invested abroad, hardly at all at home.

This is evident in the cityscape: While Dubai, Abu Dhabi, and Doha have transformed into futuristic metropolises of the global economy with enormous skyscrapers, large areas in prime locations in Kuwait lie fallow. Plaster slowly peels off the facades in the city center. Instead of rolling almost silently over wide streets as in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), cars in Kuwait occasionally rumble over potholes.

The trauma of the invasion is omnipresent. A recent exhibition titled «Heroes of the Nation» opened at a Kuwaiti gallery for modern art – in which schoolchildren grapple with the latest «external political stress,» as the gallery’s accompanying text states. A large painting with Kuwaiti flags is modeled after Pablo Picasso’s work «Guernica.» Picasso’s work addressed a massacre by the German Luftwaffe in the Spanish Civil War at the time – in Kuwait, since 1990, even the younger generation always assumes the worst.

## Economy in collapse

«The consequences of the Iraqi invasion are even reflected in the architecture,» says Hamad Alfawaz. «In Kuwait, all clients want a basement under their house – because of possible air raids.» The 42-year-old almost never builds a basement in other Gulf states.

At the moment, however, Alfawaz is not planning anything. Since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran, all his projects are stalled. In his office with its unplastered concrete walls, the few employees look at large screens displaying houses that no one is currently building.

«Business is terrible,» says the entrepreneur. «Since the outbreak of war, no one wants to invest anymore, and we can no longer import some of our building materials.» Since the Iranians closed the Strait of Hormuz, Kuwait can only be supplied by land. «A container from Europe or China might have cost 2,000 dollars before the war,» Alfawaz complains. «Now it’s 10,000 dollars.»

For Tehran, Kuwait is an easy target due to its geographical proximity. The Iranian regime also cites the American military presence as a reason for the attacks. Over 13,000 U.S. soldiers are still stationed in the small country, the largest American contingent in the region.

Iran also faces few consequences when it shells the small emirate. Although the Wall Street Journal recently reported that Kuwait had itself launched attacks against Iran in response to the attacks, the emirate immediately denied the newspaper’s report. Diplomatic circles in Kuwait also consider it unlikely that the city-state actually intervened in the war itself.

Iran is also likely to pursue a strategy of division: By ceasing its attacks on Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar, but continuing to shell Kuwait and Bahrain, it weakens the unity of the Gulf Arabs. The large neighbors are unlikely to go to war for small Kuwait. The emirate remains on its own, and Iran sets a deterrent example for the remaining Gulf states in Kuwait.

Despite the attacks and the forced export halt, Kuwait lands softly: The oil-blessed country remains one of the richest in the world, with the Kuwaiti sovereign wealth fund managing over a trillion dollars. More than 15 billion of Kuwait’s petrodollars are invested in Switzerland alone. But fresh money is now scarce.

Oil production has virtually come to a standstill. Only what is needed for domestic consumption is extracted from the ground. Exports have completely ceased, because Kuwait never anticipated a blockade of the Strait of Hormuz – the country does not have pipelines to Saudi Arabia or Iraq.

## Is a Kuwaiti scenario threatening the entire Gulf?

«We must build these pipelines as quickly as possible,» says economics professor Nayef Alshammari. «A situation like before the war, where 20% of global oil exports pass through the Strait of Hormuz, must never happen again.» The economist and former Kuwaiti diplomat in Washington sits in a long white robe, typical for Gulf Arabs, in his office at Kuwait University in the south of the city. An alternative must also be found, Alshammari believes, even if the war were to end immediately. «Our trust in Iran is less than 1%.»

*Kuwaiti economist Nayef Alshammari calls for reforms after the war.* (NZZ)

And just like all other Gulf states, Kuwait urgently needs to become more independent from the oil business, according to the economist. While Qatar hosts huge sports events, the UAE has two profitable airlines, and Saudi Arabia is launching all sorts of megaprojects with its Vision 2030, Kuwait lags behind.

In the past, attempts to diversify the economy were thwarted by the constantly blocked parliament, sources in the emirate say. Emir Mishal al-Sabah, who has ruled since 2023, dissolved the popular assembly two years ago and can now decide everything alone as he sees fit. But even he has so far left major issues like the massive subsidies for his own population untouched. Kuwaitis still receive electricity, water, and gasoline almost for free. Kuwait remains the most sluggish emirate among the dynamic small states in the Gulf.

The history of the city-state is a cautionary tale for the entire region: A rapid ascent was followed by a shock and then years of stagnation. The rulers in Doha, Abu Dhabi, and Riyadh are likely to be driven by the fear that the Iran war could have a similarly profound long-term impact on their countries as the Iraqi invasion once had on Kuwait.

## Will Kuwait break away from the U.S.?

«We always wanted to be the Switzerland of the Middle East,» says Saad bin Tiflah with a sigh on the light sofa in his air-conditioned villa. «But unfortunately, our fate is different: We are the good guys in a very bad neighborhood.»

![Kuwait’s former Communications Minister Saad bin Tiflah does not believe his country will turn away from the U.S.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/07/29/7c7b3ab6-7b83-4882-992c-3ede90e81288.jpg?crop=422,591,x0,y0)
*Kuwait’s former Communications Minister Saad bin Tiflah does not believe his country will turn away from the U.S.* (NZZ)

Tiflah was Kuwait’s Communications Minister in the late 1990s – he had to polish up the image of the struggling state back then. Today, he is a welcome guest on Arab television and remains closely connected with the Kuwaiti leadership and the strong men of the region.

Small Kuwait is threatened with being crushed by two messianic ideologies in the Middle East, says Tiflah: «Radical Zionism of Israel and archaic Islamism of Iran.» «And unfortunately, Iran currently thinks that we are easy prey.»

Tehran claims that it primarily attacks American bases in the country and that attacks against its country are launched from Kuwait. So, should Kuwait break away from its former U.S. liberators to avoid future attacks?

Saad bin Tiflah brusquely dismisses this scenario: «The U.S. is the reason for our country’s stability over the past 60 years,» says the former minister. «We will continue to need them.» Other Kuwaitis, however, privately doubt the close alignment with the U.S. A former military official, who still advises Kuwait’s government, grumbles in a confidential conversation about the allies in Washington, saying their bases have only brought chaos instead of security to Kuwait.

The entire Gulf is already overhauling its security policy. Relations with the U.S. are at the top of the agenda. Kuwait is likely to do what it has been doing for 36 years now: wait to see how the other Gulf states position themselves, make a decision carefully, and avoid attracting attention.
