# Setback for the right-wing populists in the Netherlands: The social liberals win the parliamentary election

*Two years ago, the PVV rose to become the largest party in the Netherlands. Now, the social-liberal party D66 has won the parliamentary election. Rob Jetten, the party leader, could become prime minister.*

Where does right-wing populism stand in the eyes of Western European voters? This question is of particular concern to representatives of centrist parties. After all, right-wing populist movements are primarily stealing voters from them. The Netherlands is a good indicator of the political mood, as politicians who focus on the issue of migration and thus attract attention have been active there for 25 years.

The Dutch parliamentary elections on Wednesday received considerable attention. The last elections in November 2023 were won by Geert Wilders' right-wing populist Party for Freedom (PVV). At that time, they more than doubled their number of seats to 37 and became part of a four-party government.

After Wednesday's election night, the exact result remained unclear for a long time. The social-liberal party D66 was locked in a close race with Wilders' PVV. After approximately 99 percent of the votes had been counted, D66's lead was around 15,000 votes.

According to the ANP news agency, D66 has held an insurmountable lead since Friday midday. The party also has a chance of winning a residual seat, which would give it 27 of the 150 seats in parliament. Wilders' PVV won 26 seats – a significant loss compared to the 2023 election, when the party became the largest force with 37 seats. D66 leader Rob Jetten, only 38 years old, now has a chance of becoming prime minister.

## Wilders pushed the coalition forward with the asylum issue

Wednesday's elections became necessary because Wilders had broken up the then-governing, ideologically diverse four-party coalition in June. It had only been in power for eleven months and had fractured over asylum issues.

Wilders' right-liberal and centrist coalition partners had indeed gone to great lengths to accommodate the demagogue on this issue. In their coalition agreement, they declared an «asylum crisis» and agreed on some rather harsh measures. These included, for example, suspending the processing of asylum applications or prohibiting refugees from bringing their families to the Netherlands should the problem worsen.

Despite such concessions, Wilders, who was not a member of the government, pushed the coalition on the issue of asylum. He demanded, for example, that no more asylum seekers be allowed into the country. His coalition partners, of course, could not agree to this, and so, at the beginning of June, Wilders announced on the news channel X that the PVV ministers he had delegated were leaving the government.

## There are other concerns besides just the asylum system

The four-party coalition had acquired a rather poor reputation. This was partly due to the chaotic governance, primarily caused by Wilders' unpredictability. The Dutch were also dissatisfied because the coalition's track record proved meager.

While migration has been a constant political issue, other matters are of at least equal concern to the Dutch. Firstly, young people complain about the difficulty of finding housing. The government has failed to meet its housing construction targets. Secondly, it has failed in its plan to initiate the construction of several nuclear power plants. Thirdly, the Netherlands has been exceeding nitrogen emission limits for years. Around 50 percent of these emissions originate from the agricultural sector. The government has also failed to find a long-term solution to this problem.

## Difficult coalition talks

The Netherlands is likely facing months of coalition talks. The last government was only formed after protracted, six-month negotiations.

The leaders of the four major parties, GLP/PvdA, D66, CDA, and VVD, emphasized before the elections that they would not form a coalition with Wilders. However, a common enemy does not automatically create a stable government. Moreover, Dilan Yesilgöz, the president of the long-established right-liberal VVD party, also announced that she would not form a coalition with Frans Timmermans.

The leader of the Green-Social Democratic Alliance (GLP/PvdA) is the most famous Dutch politician. He was once a member of the European Commission and is considered the father of the now controversial Green Deal, which aims to make Europe a climate-neutral continent.

Timmermans is not particularly popular in the Netherlands, and his ideology differs considerably from that of the economically liberal VVD party. However, he announced his resignation as party president on Wednesday evening. He had dreamed of becoming prime minister, but after the party's seat losses, that office was out of reach. His resignation could facilitate the formation of a coalition.

Because 15 parties will be represented in parliament and the PVV is not slated to be part of the government, at least four political associations, one on the left and one on the right of center, will have to join forces to form a government. D66, VVD, the Christian Democratic CDA, and GLP/PvdA would together have a majority of 86 seats.

However, such a coalition will not be formed without concessions to the political opposition. The housing and energy shortages, as well as the nitrogen problem, will remain ongoing issues. It seems that the majority of the Dutch population wants a more proactive government than the previous one. Above all, they want it to be more reliable.

«Millions of Dutch people have said goodbye to a politics of negativity,» said D66 leader Jetten. Wilders, meanwhile, claimed that being in opposition was more fun than being in government. Many Dutch people seem to have seen through the demagogue on this point.
