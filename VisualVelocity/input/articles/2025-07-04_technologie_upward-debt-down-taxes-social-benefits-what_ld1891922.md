# Upward debt, down taxes and social benefits: what the «Big Beautiful Bill» means for Americans

*In the end, they were subservient to Trump once again: The Republicans have pushed a massive reform package through Congress. Who wins and who loses?*

Despite all internal party concerns and criticism, Republicans in the House of Representatives passed the «Big Beautiful Bill» on Thursday afternoon (local time). Just in time for Independence Day on Friday, the «BBB,» as the reform is known in Washington circles, landed on the president's desk—just as he had hoped. Ultimately, the fear of publicly incurring the president's wrath likely prevailed.

The mammoth 940-page bill affects countless areas of life and is the most significant achievement to date of the Republican Party with its trifecta – holding both houses of Congress and the White House. Above all, it is one of the most far-reaching and expensive legislative packages passed by Congress in recent years.

## What are the most important changes?

With this massive reform package, Republicans are fulfilling Trump's biggest campaign promises in one fell swoop: his 2017 tax cuts, which would otherwise have expired at the end of the year, are enshrined in law; he receives significantly more funding for the military, border security, and protection against illegal immigration; and taxes on tips and overtime are eliminated.

To finance this, the government is drastically cutting social programs, particularly Medicaid and food assistance (see below). However, this is insufficient to cover the costs, so the US is significantly raising its debt ceiling by another $5.1 trillion to more than $41 trillion. As a result, tax revenues are likely to decline for decades to come.

What is particularly noteworthy is the political calculation that went into the timing of the reforms: The most generous tax cuts are being granted this year. The most painful cuts, however, will only take effect after the 2026 midterm elections, when the future control of both houses of Congress is at stake. With this clever tactic, Republicans hope to temporarily defuse voter backlash against the cuts.

## Which taxes are changing?

During his first term, Trump pushed through several tax cuts, including the introduction of lower income tax brackets. These changes have now been permanently enshrined in law. While this will result in significantly lower revenues for the government, from the taxpayers' perspective it simply maintains the status quo.

That's why Trump is now handing out new tax breaks to practically all citizens. 84 percent of all households in the US will have to pay less tax next year, according to calculations by the Tax Policy Center think tank .

Some examples: Tips and overtime pay will be largely tax-exempt in the future. Retirees will receive a $6,000 higher tax-free allowance. Families can already deduct a slightly higher amount from their tax liability starting this tax year, specifically $2,200 per child (the US does not have a child benefit).

Patriotism is also being encouraged: Anyone who buys a car made in America can deduct up to $10,000 in interest on their car loan from their tax liability. Buying vehicles on credit is very common in the US. Another perk: Americans born between 2025 and 2028 will receive a $1,000 «Trump account» from the government, in which their parents can then save money tax-free. Several tax breaks for businesses will also be permanently enshrined in law.

In the future, Americans will be able to claim a significantly larger portion of their state taxes against their federal income tax ($40,000 instead of the current $10,000). And the inheritance and gift tax exemption will increase from nearly $14 million to $15 million for single individuals and double that amount for married couples. Only a very small percentage of Americans will benefit from this.

## Who are the winners?

Even though Trump is handing out gifts to everyone, the biggest ones are clearly going to the wealthy. According to an analysis by the nonpartisan think tank Tax Policy Center, those income brackets that benefit most from the changes are the top 20 percent of earners (earning $217,000 or more). Their tax burden will decrease by an average of $12,500 – equivalent to 3.4 percent of their after-tax income.

The bottom 20 percent, who earn $35,000 or less annually, only benefit from the reforms by $150 – equivalent to 0.8 percent of their previous after-tax income.

In other words, 60 percent of the tax cuts go to the top 20 percent of households, and more than a third to those earning $460,000 or more. Other independent think tanks, such as the Yale Budget Lab , also conclude in their analyses that the wealthiest in the country will benefit most from the reform.

These calculations do not yet include the additional income losses that low-income earners face due to cuts in Medicaid health insurance and food stamps.

How much a taxpayer benefits depends heavily on where they live, according to an analysis by the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy. Generally speaking, already wealthy families in conservative states that already tax the wealthy sparingly benefit the most. In particular, the wealthiest households in Wyoming, South Dakota, and Texas are now saving more than $100,000 in taxes thanks to the «Big Beautiful Bill.»

## Who are the losers?

The losers in the reforms are the poorest Americans. Those who currently receive food stamps from the government—like 42 million families—or are covered by the government's Medicaid health insurance program will face stricter requirements in the future: more documentation, no automatic renewal of benefits, and stricter work requirements. For example, those with children over 14 will have to work 80 hours per month to remain eligible for benefits.

This will result in the lowest-income 10 percent of American households having $1,600 less per year in the future,as calculated by the Congressional Budget Office – almost 4 percent of their annual income.

Certain groups of foreigners also lose out as a result of the reform: refugees and asylum seekers are no longer entitled to food assistance, health insurance, and other government services. Furthermore, fees for things like filing asylum applications in the US, immigration hearings, and work permits are increasing.

Those who work in the service industry but earn less in tips than can already be deducted as standard will not benefit from the changes brought about by the new «No tax on tips» rule.

Private universities will also have to dig deeper into their pockets in the future: While they are tax-exempt, they pay taxes on their endowments, i.e., the donations accumulated over the years. These taxes are currently very low at 1.4 percent. Now, the rate for the wealthiest universities is rising to a hefty 8 percent. This will particularly affect wealthy institutions like Stanford, Yale, and Harvard, with which the government is already embroiled in dispute.

Students also face restrictions: The government loans they can take out for their studies will be capped at a lower amount. And loan repayments can no longer be temporarily suspended if someone becomes unemployed or falls into financial hardship.

Renewable energy providers are also at a disadvantage: The tax break of over $7,500 for electric cars or the installation of solar panels and heat pumps will be partially eliminated in just a few weeks, and the tax benefits for companies will be phased out starting next year.

However, the biggest losers are likely to be future generations: National debt in the USA will rise enormously in the next ten years, so today's tax breaks will come at the expense of tomorrow's taxpayers.

## What will happen to the social programs?

To finance the tax cuts, Republicans are slashing several social programs. First, they are attacking President Barack Obama's much-maligned healthcare reform: tax deductions that citizens could claim for their health insurance premiums under Obama's reform are now being eliminated.

Secondly, they are making it more difficult to claim food assistance: The work requirements and the documentation needed to participate in the nation's largest aid program, Snap, are being tightened. However, studies show that the bureaucratic hurdles, in particular, are often too high for those in need to overcome—especially those with limited education. Critics believe this is precisely what Republicans intend: Medicaid and Snap will soon cost the state less because they are pushing eligible recipients out of the programs—not because they are uncovering widespread fraud.

Thirdly, the government's social program Medicaid is to be cut in particular: The aim is to save the impressive sum of 1 trillion dollars in the coming years.

Medicaid, established in 1965 under President Lyndon Johnson as part of his major social reform, remains the largest health insurer in the United States. Nearly 78 million Americans – roughly one in four – are insured through it: the homeless and other vulnerable people, people with disabilities, pregnant women, and a large number of children. In fiscal year 2022, children accounted for 36 percent of Medicaid recipients .

Under President Barack Obama's 2010 healthcare reform, eligibility was expanded so that virtually all low-income earners under 65 now receive health insurance through Medicaid. This can include employees in the restaurant and hospitality industry who are not covered by their employer's health insurance. Some states, such as California and New York, also offer Medicaid to undocumented immigrants; Washington plans to financially penalize them for doing so in the future.

President Trump assured voters during his election campaign and in recent interviews that he only wanted to curb Medicaid abuse and fraud. However, experts predict that 12 million Americans—one in six Medicaid-insured individuals—will lose this coverage by 2034. Currently, approximately 8 percent of Americans lack health insurance.

## What do Americans think about the law?

President Trump has been praising the proposed legislation for weeks as a victory for the American people. However, in virtually all polls, a majority of citizens view it critically.

KFF did not specify how large the proportion of the two factions was within the surveyed Republicans – that is, how significant the Maga supporters are within the party.
