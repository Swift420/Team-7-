# Shock follows shock: Now a super El Niño threatens the global economy

*The climate phenomenon is expected to be particularly severe in 2026. Following the energy shock caused by the Iran war, even higher food prices and increased logistical problems are now looming.*

The economy will remain unsettled in 2026, with one crisis following another. First, the war in the Persian Gulf drove up prices for oil and oil-derived products like diesel and fertilizer worldwide. Then came a heat wave and drought in Europe. As a result, the wheat harvest in France, for example, is likely to be poor. Due to low water levels on the Rhine and other important waterways, ships are able to carry less and less cargo.

Now the economy faces another blow: the El Niño weather phenomenon. Researchers predict it will be particularly strong in 2026. This climatic phenomenon, observed for centuries, builds up over the Pacific every few years, usually reaching its peak around Christmas time, and disrupts air currents and precipitation patterns worldwide. As a result, drought, heat, storms, and floods can damage infrastructure in many parts of the world, significantly disrupt supply chains, and lead to crop failures from South America to Africa and Asia.

## From price shocks to social unrest

Economically, a lot is at stake: A super El Niño, which experts currently predict with an 81% probability, would hit the economy at a moment when it has hardly any buffer to cushion a shock, as a Deutsche Bank analyst recently noted.

The effects would be felt through multiple channels: higher food prices, rising energy prices and increased supply chain disruptions. Emerging economies would be particularly vulnerable, and social unrest could even erupt there.

According to researchers at Dartmouth College in the US, an El Niño event causes worldwide income losses of several trillion dollars. While such projections should be treated with caution, they do illustrate the potential scale of the damage.

Heat-related crop losses and logistical problems caused by severe weather would fuel price increases. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) anticipates that the climate phenomenon will cause commodity prices (excluding energy) to rise by approximately 5.5% within a year.

In the U.S., prices for agricultural commodities on the futures markets have already risen sharply since the beginning of the year: by over 40% for rice, more than 30% for wheat, and over 25% for cotton. However, the prevailing and expected weather conditions were only one factor among several.

The extreme weather is already impacting cocoa prices: Since meteorologists warned of the super El Niño last month, the price of cocoa has risen by 50%. This is bad news for Swiss chocolate manufacturers like Lindt & Sprüngli and Barry Callebaut, as it increases costs.

At the same time, rice prices also surged. The white grains are the staple food in many Asian countries and therefore crucial for feeding the population. According to the British bank HSBC, Indonesia and Malaysia have already begun purchasing rice in anticipation of the looming El Niño.

Adding to the difficulties is the weak monsoon rains in India this year, which could lead to water shortages and threaten to reduce the harvest in the world's most populous country. This could also have consequences for other countries, as India is also a major exporter of rice.

According to Allianz Research, significant risks besides the rice harvest exist for sugar and palm oil due to drought, low stock levels, and potential export restrictions. The supply of cocoa and Robusta coffee could decrease, while favorable weather conditions in other regions could lead to falling prices for soybeans, corn, and Arabica coffee.

El Niño is not harmful everywhere; in some parts of the world, it can even boost harvests. The recent heat wave in Europe is one example. «The heat wave coincided precisely with the planting season, especially in parts of Southern Europe, while parts of Northern Europe benefited from the heat, as the warmth was advantageous for their planting season,» said Nikhil da Victoria Lobo, Head of Property and Casualty Insurance for Western and Southern Europe, the Middle East, and Africa at the reinsurer Swiss Re in Zurich. He noted that it is therefore difficult to predict exactly how weather patterns will affect agricultural production, as this depends on the timing of the event. This also applies to the consequences of a strong El Niño, he added.

## Fertilizer shortage as an additional problem

Especially for emerging economies around the globe, a super El Niño would be a second severe blow: First, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz had serious consequences for raw material exports and the price of fertilizer produced from natural gas in the Gulf region.

Farmers in developing countries have therefore reduced their crop production. The super El Niño now threatens them with further losses. Furthermore, the recent escalation of fighting between the US and Iran could once again restrict the global supply of fertilizers. Approximately one-third of the global supply of urea, a key fertilizer ingredient, comes from the Persian Gulf.

Added to this are the effects of protectionist measures. Particularly with food, governments are often willing to introduce price controls, build up strategic reserves, or restrict exports to ensure the supply for their own populations. However, on the world market, this can, in the worst-case scenario, create shortages for import-dependent countries and lead to a further surge in inflation. According to the Wall Street bank Goldman Sachs, a particularly large number of protectionist trade barriers have been erected for agricultural commodities in recent years.

The typhoon season in Asia is also expected to be more severe, as El Niño promotes the formation of cyclones at sea. For example, Typhoon Doksuri raged through China, Taiwan, Vietnam, and the Philippines in July 2023. The German reinsurer Munich Re estimates the resulting damage at around $25 billion.

Combined with ongoing climate change, a strong El Niño is likely to pose a challenge for Central Europe as well. The insurance industry is already preparing for a wave of claims. At the same time, rising prices for agricultural products threaten to further fuel already elevated inflation.

Economists at Oxford Economics even estimate that the recent summer heat would drive up food prices more sharply next year than the war in Ukraine, a major wheat producer. El Niño could further amplify this trend, increasing food price inflation by one percentage point, the consulting firm writes.

Furthermore, damage from natural disasters is likely to increase further. According to Swiss Re, insured losses have already exceeded $100 billion annually over the past six years. Part of this increase is attributable to climate change. However, other factors also play a role, such as demographics.

This is boosting business for reinsurers. Covering risks from hurricanes and floods remains the primary task for companies like Swiss Re. According to Victoria Lobo, extreme heat, on the other hand, represents an invisible threat that affects life itself, productivity and people's health. He adds that the effects often only become apparent after a delay. A heat wave, which can be intensified by El Niño, also exacerbates natural phenomena such as forest fires or floods and causes damage to infrastructure.
