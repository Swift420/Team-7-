The vaccination requirements for children in Florida public schools would be abolished, announced Joseph Ladapo, head of the state's health department, in early August. «All of them. Every single one of them is wrong and reeks of contempt and slavery.»

Currently, in Florida, as in all US states, certain vaccinations are mandatory for children attending daycare or school. Such mandatory vaccinations are the exception in Europe: Switzerland has no such requirement, and Germany has only had mandatory measles vaccinations for school children for the past five years. In the US, immunizations against measles, mumps, and rubella are required, as well as against diphtheria, polio, and tetanus. This requirement is not absolute; exceptions for religious or personal reasons are possible in most states.

## The Maha movement is a gathering place for vaccine opponents

In fact, most experts in Europe are also critical of mandatory vaccinations. However, the initiative in Florida is part of a general policy shift under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, under the motto «Make America Healthy Again,» or Maha for short, which increasingly questions the benefits of vaccinations.

Even though it's not yet clear how the new Maha policy will develop in the coming years, Kennedy and the current American administration will no longer be pioneers of vaccination. This is likely to result in declining vaccination rates. What consequences could this have in America or even worldwide?

Currently, vaccination rates in the USA are comparable to those in Switzerland or Germany, as the example of measles illustrates.

However, even vaccination rates of over 90 percent are epidemiologically too low to stop the spread of the disease. This was demonstrated by the outbreak centered in Texas, in which almost a thousand people – most of them unvaccinated children – contracted measles in the first half of 2025. Two children and one adult died; these were the first deaths in the US in a decade.

## Herd immunity: why high vaccination rates are often not high enough

At first glance, the measles vaccination rates in the USA (92.7 percent), Germany (93.2 percent), and Switzerland (94.0 percent) appear quite high. However, the fact that even these figures are not sufficient is primarily due to two epidemiological phenomena.

The first problem is most easily illustrated with a numbers game. Measles is extremely contagious: In an unvaccinated group, each person with measles infects roughly fifteen other people. These, in turn, infect fifteen more – thanks to this extreme multiplication, the virus infects every single person in the group within a very short time.

Experts refer to this as a basic reproduction number, or R-value, of 15. With a vaccination rate of 90 percent, this value does decrease to 1.5, because most contacts are now protected from the virus and each infected person statistically only infects 1.5 healthy people. However, even this is still more than enough for the virus to spread like wildfire.

Only when the effective R-value falls below 1.0 do chains of infection come to a halt – a state known as herd immunity. For the highly infectious measles, this threshold is theoretically a vaccination rate of around 93 percent; for other, less infectious diseases, correspondingly lower values ​​suffice. However, vaccination strategies aimed at herd immunity always strive for a higher buffer – for example, Switzerland and Germany have a target value of 95 percent for measles vaccination.

## Local gaps in vaccination coverage are sufficient for larger outbreaks

This stems from problem number two: A national or even regional average says little about vaccination rates in individual regions. For example, the relatively high measles vaccination rate of 94 percent in Texas was insufficient to stop the outbreak in the spring. This was because the outbreak was concentrated among members of the Mennonite religious community. Many Mennonites reject vaccinations. Consequently, vaccination rates are low both geographically in regions with a widespread presence of this evangelical free church and socially within the Mennonite community and its schools.

This problem also affects Germany and Switzerland, where some regions are significantly below the national average. Examples include the canton of Appenzell Innerrhoden, northern Baden-Württemberg, and parts of Saxony. Here, too, worldview likely plays a role. Both Appenzell Innerrhoden and the Stuttgart region are considered strongholds of vaccine-skeptical alternative medicine.

The latest report from the Robert Koch Institute (RKI) in Berlin on vaccination rates in Germany concludes that while overall these rates are satisfactory, significant gaps in vaccination coverage still exist locally.

## Neglectful parents cause gaps in vaccination coverage

Active anti-vaccination sentiment is one thing. Even more serious, however, is simple negligence on the part of parents. Many children receive their vaccinations significantly later than recommended. This is especially true for the second or third dose, which is crucial for optimal protection with many vaccines and is often completely missed. The Robert Koch Institute (RKI) also observes similar gaps in vaccination coverage among the elderly, for whom vaccinations against influenza, pneumococcus, and shingles, for example, are recommended.

Overall, vaccination rates in both Germany and Switzerland have declined slightly in recent years, albeit from a high level – a trend also observed by the World Health Organization (WHO) across Europe . The WHO sees this as increasing the risk of local outbreaks, particularly of measles and pertussis (whooping cough).

## The triumphant march of the polio vaccine stopped just before reaching its goal

Polio is also a major concern for many experts, as it used to confine many children to wheelchairs or even require an iron lung for life. Around the turn of the millennium, the disease caused by the poliovirus was on the verge of eradication – a success of the oral polio vaccine with attenuated viruses, which had only been introduced in the early 1960s. Until then, this had only been achieved once before, with smallpox, which was officially eradicated in 1979. However, infections are now increasing again worldwide, and eradication is a distant prospect.

How can current vaccination rates be maintained, or better yet, increased? Government-mandated vaccination requirements, like the one Joseph Ladapo is now criticizing in Florida, are controversial even among experts. Most of them advocate for other approaches, such as building trust through education about the benefits and risks of vaccinations, or effective systems to remind parents of missed vaccinations.

The US is moving away from rising vaccination rates for the time being. A direct spillover of disease outbreaks to Europe is therefore not expected as long as vaccination rates here remain high. However, unfounded anti-vaccine sentiments can also be contagious. The best way to combat this is through immunization through honest education.
