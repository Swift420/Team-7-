# Korea, Cyprus, Nagorno-Karabakh: what Ukraine can learn from frozen conflicts

*Even if the guns finally fall silent in Ukraine, the conflict will not be over. Instead of peace, a precarious ceasefire could characterize the coming decades, as experience from other wars shows.*

The term originates from the period following the collapse of the Soviet empire: «frozen conflicts» was the name given to the precarious situation between war and peace that prevailed after fierce fighting on the southern edge of the former USSR at the beginning of the 1990s.

There, formerly autonomous regions (Transnistria in Moldova, Nagorno-Karabakh in Azerbaijan, South Ossetia and Abkhazia in Georgia), which distrusted the newly independent nation-states, had declared their independence. They were supported in this by Russia. Thanks to its influence over the separatists, Russia weakened the newly independent states and thus attempted to keep them within its sphere of influence. The Kremlin's imperial instinct had never ceased to function.

A frozen conflict differs from war in that outbreaks of violence occur only sporadically, if at all. It differs from peace in the absence of an international treaty between the two sides. Political scientists emphasize that freezing a conflict does not create a stable state. The conflict can thaw and erupt again. Furthermore, third-party states usually play an important role in frozen conflicts: as «patrons» of one side, as allies, or as mediators.

Researchers at the University of Prague have compiled a list of over forty frozen conflicts worldwide since 1949. While some entries may be debatable, this is certainly not just a post-Soviet phenomenon, but a global type of conflict.

Many observers expect Russia's war against Ukraine—which was temporarily frozen between the annexation of Crimea in 2014 and the major offensive in 2022—to end without a victor. It is far more likely that it will be frozen along a ceasefire line. It is therefore worthwhile to examine three very different frozen conflicts. From this, conclusions can be drawn for the future of Ukraine.

## Nagorno-Karabakh: Strategic patience pays off

During Soviet times, the predominantly Armenian-populated region of Nagorno-Karabakh was an autonomous territory within the Azerbaijan Soviet Socialist Republic. During the collapse of the Soviet Union, it declared its independence. Nagorno-Karabakh, with Armenian support, was able to defend itself militarily against Azerbaijani forces and even expand its territory. Expulsions and massacres occurred between the Christian Armenians and the Muslim Azerbaijanis. In 1994, a ceasefire brokered by the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) ended the conflict.

Azerbaijan had effectively lost territory, but Karabakh's independence was not internationally recognized. Despite tensions and occasional skirmishes, the ceasefire held for a quarter of a century. Russia played a crucial role for both sides during those years; they were heavily dependent on Moscow militarily and, to some extent, economically.

But the Azerbaijani leadership refused to accept the situation. They opted for time, economic progress, and rearmament – ​​practicing strategic patience. Unlike in the isolated landlocked country of Armenia, the economic upswing came thanks to natural gas and oil exports: Between 1995 and 2022, the economy grew 45-fold (Armenia: 15-fold), and military spending increased 29-fold (in Armenia: 13-fold).

The army was modernized with Israeli and Turkish assistance, including the acquisition of combat drones. In 2020, Azerbaijan recaptured parts of the breakaway territory. In 2023—with Russia, the Armenians' protector, tied down in Ukraine—the second blow followed. The remainder of Nagorno-Karabakh was seized in short order. The Armenian defeat was complete and devastating. The entire Armenian population of the region fled or was expelled. Although a peace agreement between Azerbaijan and Armenia is still pending, the conflict over Karabakh has at least been resolved.

## Cyprus and the EU's missed opportunity

In 1974, a coup attempt by nationalist Greeks in Cyprus, who wanted to unite the island with mainland Greece, failed. Then, as now, approximately 80 percent of Cyprus's population was of Greek Cypriot descent and 20 percent of Turkish Cypriot descent.

To prevent the island's unification with Greece, Turkish troops landed in Cyprus in July 1974 and occupied the northern part of the island (37 percent). Expulsions occurred on both sides. Since then, Greeks and Turks have lived largely segregated in the south and north, respectively, and the conflict remains frozen.

A ceasefire agreement was signed later that same year, establishing a demarcation line («Green Line») monitored by a UN military mission. After proposals from various states and the UN failed, the parliament of the occupied north proclaimed the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus in November 1983.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 937dd499597158b63c8944b41d6677d9]

In the half-century since the division, various attempts at reunification have failed. The most promising was the Annan Plan, named after the then UN Secretary-General. Crucially, in the 1990s, the entire island simultaneously had a realistic prospect of EU membership.

The Commission rightly assumed that this prospect would increase the willingness to compromise in both parts of the island. Annan's plan envisioned a confederation (United Republic of Cyprus) whose constituent parts would enjoy considerable autonomy. However, in referendums held in the two parts of the island, only the Turkish Cypriots approved the compromise (with 65 percent). The south clearly rejected it (75 percent). For most Greek Cypriots, the equal rights of the minority, which comprised only 20 percent of the population, went too far. They feared the influence of Ankara and political gridlock.

A decisive factor in the failure was that Brussels' promise of accession was binding in any case, both for reunification and for the south, the Republic of Cyprus. Since then, Brussels has had little influence on resolving the conflict. Precisely because the situation remains calm along the buffer zone, the two parts of the island have become increasingly independent. The division of the island appears to be cemented for the foreseeable future.

## Korea: The nuclear weapon prolongs the ice age

Even older than the conflict in Cyprus is the frozen conflict on the Korean Peninsula. Nevertheless, the situation there is more fragile than in the eastern Mediterranean. Korea had been a unified political space for centuries until the peninsula was annexed by the Japanese in 1910.

After Tokyo's surrender in 1945, the country was divided into a Soviet occupation zone in the north and an American one in the south. This resulted in two states in 1948, which found themselves on opposing sides in the emerging Cold War: the communist Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea) and the authoritarian, pro-American Republic of Korea (South Korea).

In June 1950, North Korea attacked the South to force reunification. A coalition army, led by the US and authorized by a UN mandate, fought back. The war claimed three to four million lives and ended in 1953 with an armistice at the starting point of the fighting, the 38th parallel.

A buffer zone 250 kilometers long and 4 kilometers wide was established along this line. The armistice was signed by the USA, China, and North Korea; South Korea refused to sign, as it rejected the division. It has since stationed American troops on its territory and concluded a mutual assistance agreement with Washington in 1953.

This ceasefire alone demonstrates that the conflict was not merely an internal Korean matter. During the Cold War, the peninsula became a crucial arena for the geopolitical struggle between the major powers. This continues to this day: North Korea is part of an alliance with China and Russia that seeks to curb Western influence wherever possible; in 2024, the country sent troops to war against Ukraine.

The involvement of major powers as «patrons» of one side solidifies the opposing positions because it prevents both sides from thawing the frozen conflict, whether by force or diplomacy. This has been especially true since 2006, when North Korea became a nuclear power. Sanctions and negotiations have barely changed the status quo since then.

## What does this mean for Ukraine?

The case of Nagorno-Karabakh demonstrates that when the balance of power between adversaries shifts over time, the risk increases that a frozen conflict will thaw and the stronger side will seek a decisive victory. Azerbaijan used its superior economic power to drive the enemy out of the disputed territory after 26 years with a modernized army. Ukraine therefore knows that it must use a ceasefire to invest in its defenses against Russia. This can reduce the risk of a renewed attack.

Cyprus's division makes it clear that prosperous development is indeed possible as long as the conflict remains frozen. The southern part of the island has made significant economic progress – primarily thanks to its accession to the EU. Cyprus could also offer Ukraine hope insofar as it was able to join the Union despite part of its territory being occupied.

The case of Korea makes one thing clear: the more deeply the major powers are involved in the frozen conflict, the less likely it is to thaw. But a peaceful settlement is also more difficult when many powerful actors are involved. The nuclear arsenal on both sides makes a forced reunification highly risky and unlikely.

For Ukraine (which has no nuclear umbrella), this means that recapturing the territory annexed by the nuclear power Russia is not realistic. The best possible outcome of this tragedy in Eastern Europe is probably coexistence, not peace.
