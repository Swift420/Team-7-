# Chinese safari parks fuel illegal elephant trade

*Zoo-themed safari parks are booming in China – and with them, ethically dubious elephant shows. The charismatic animals are often obtained illegally from Laos.*

Half an hour before the show begins, the seats at the edge of the elephant enclosure are filled to capacity. Fanfare sounds ring out from the loudspeakers under the ceiling. Waitresses serve ready-made noodles, fresh fruit, and iced tea. Excited visitors take photos of each other; the children can hardly wait for the spectacle to begin in Wild Elephant Valley in Xishuangbanna in the southern Chinese province of Yunnan.

At noon sharp, a guard enters the arena. Behind him, six elephants trot along in single file. Each holds the tail of the animal in front of it with its trunk. The elephants stop in the middle of the enclosure and bow.

![Visitors at an elephant show in the circus ring of a safari park in Shanghai in 2023.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/07/15/08101b33-ee58-4477-88e8-2871d75baf4b.jpeg?width=1024)

The guard pulls a bucket filled with carrots high into a tree. An elephant reacts reflexively and stands up on its hind legs. The animal uses its trunk to pull a carrot out of the bucket and hold it up to the audience. The children cheer.

Wild Elephant Valley is located not far from the border with Laos, and is one of the largest elephant parks in the country. According to local authorities, the park attracted almost 10 million visitors between 2016 and 2022 . Over those six years, the city of Xishuangbanna, which owns Wild Elephant Valley, generated revenue equivalent to 66 million Swiss francs ($81.9 million) from ticket sales alone.

## China's government has big plans

Zoos and safari parks such as Wild Elephant Valley are a magnet for visitors. In 2022, 94 million Chinese people visited zoos and safari parks. This represented an increase of 16% compared to the previous year. The total revenue generated by the 195 parks nationwide amounted to 775 million Swiss francs in the same period, 12% more than in 2021.

![Tourists feed elephants in Wild Elephant Valley on World Elephant Day on Aug. 12, 2022.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/07/31/df24d626-97dc-4673-8de5-c6feb53c7161.jpg?width=1024)
*Tourists feed elephants in Wild Elephant Valley on World Elephant Day on Aug. 12, 2022.* (VCG/Getty)

Like Wild Elephant Valley, almost all safari parks are operated by cities, counties, and provinces. It's the same as always in China: Wherever there's big money to be made, the state is almost always pulling the strings. That is why China plans to open hundreds of zoos and safari parks in the coming years.

New facilities are often a mixture of amusement park and zoo. In addition to elephants, there are also roller coasters and water slides. Guests stay in themed hotels, and trains and gondolas transport visitors through the park grounds.

Admission to these zoos often costs 50 francs or more. And once one passes through the park gate, they have to pay again. If a visitor to Wild Elephant Valley wants to get close to the elephants, they must first purchase a fruit basket for 4 Swiss francs. Visitors can feed the animals with carrots, bananas, and apples.

But what appears colorful, chic, and dazzling from the outside is actually a dirty business. The park operators need animals, but can hardly meet the rapidly growing demand through legal means – so they break the law. Safari parks often obtain the animals from neighboring Laos through convoluted channels.

![Three elephants and their keepers in Wild Elephant Valley.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/07/31/913ff147-e36a-4dde-9870-794d781a0bd3.jpg?width=1024)
*Three elephants and their keepers in Wild Elephant Valley.* (VCG / Getty Images)

A European zoo expert who has advised Chinese parks and wishes to remain anonymous is concerned. If all the animals for the new facilities come from the wild, the forests will soon be empty - even before they are cut down.

## An elephant from Laos for one million dollars

Boten, about two hours' drive south of Xishuangbanna, is a rundown village on the Laotian side of the border. The city has gained notoriety for gambling, drug trafficking, and prostitution. Here, Chinese people enjoy everything that is forbidden in their homeland. The streets are lined with Chinese restaurants, and Chinese is spoken everywhere. People pay with yuan.

Boten is also a hub for the illegal trade in elephants from Laos. Laotian customs offices are located on a dusty road on the outskirts of the small town. Heavily loaded trucks rumble up the narrow road toward customs. Many of them bring fruit and vegetables from Thailand to China.

At the border, behind the customs post, there is a forest path that is used to bring elephants to the Chinese side. The customs officers look the other way.

The elephant costs $100,000. Added to this are the costs of quarantine, training the elephants, and obtaining Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, or CITES, permits – including bribes. All in all, a zoo pays around $1 million for an elephant like this. «The Chinese agents who organize the whole thing are making a lot of money,» says Karl Ammann. The environmentalist has been working on elephant smuggling from Laos to China for years.

The NZZ has arranged to meet with the director of a travel agency in Vientiane. The company primarily earns its money from tourism. However, a subsidiary is also active in the import-export business. We pretend to want to buy an elephant for a safari park in Tianjin, China, on behalf of a Chinese operator.

Yes, in the past, elephants have repeatedly been moved from Laos to China on behalf of Chinese companies, explains the director, adding that he can help. However, we would have to pay substantial sums in bribes. The government, customs officials, the forestry commission – many palms need to be greased. The director is vague about the possible total.

Then he calls a colleague, the in-house expert on smuggling elephants to China. After a brief conversation, the director informs us of the elephant expert's verdict: it is considerably more difficult today than it was a few years ago to transport an elephant to China. One must get to higher levels in the Laotian government – ​​up to deputy prime minister, he says. It's difficult at the moment. Perhaps he could help again in the future.

An employee at the Elephant Conservation Center in Xayaburi, Laos, confirms that illegal elephant trading has declined somewhat, but has by no means come to a halt. In the past, the Elephant Conservation Center has repeatedly managed to rescue elephants brought from Laos to China and kept there under questionable circumstances.

## Questionable shows disguised as educational events

The crowd roars in Wild Elephant Valley. The keeper has put on a doctor's coat and is inserting a thermometer into an elephant's anus. Visitors are asked to guess the animal's body temperature; «36.5 degrees,» the guard says loudly. The enclosure where this show takes place is called Elephant Science Park. The park operators want to give the impression that the elephant shows are educational events.

![In 2021, the elephants in Wild Elephant Valley still had to play soccer.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/07/31/b0bf1da8-ee45-44c9-8d84-413975b81eee.jpg?width=1024)
*In 2021, the elephants in Wild Elephant Valley still had to play soccer.* (VCG/Getty)

Chinese authorities have recently tightened supervision of safari parks in response to public complaints. The worst abuses in elephant training, husbandry, and cruelty during shows have been eliminated. For example, the animals no longer have to play soccer.

The improvement in the situation is also due to an inspection carried out by CITES at parks in southern China in 2023. The organization is responsible for regulating international wildlife trade and also visited Wild Elephant Valley. It recommended suspending commercial trade in virtually all CITES-listed animal and plant species from Laos. The organization upheld this recommendation at this year's annual meeting in Geneva.

CITES confirms on request that it repeatedly receives reports of corruption. Although the organization can only issue recommendations and requests to its 180 member countries, the system enjoys a good reputation in the European zoo scene. At least countries can be put under political pressure in the event of violations.

This becomes more difficult in gray areas. Some safari parks lease elephants from Laos. The contract specifies how long a safari park is allowed to use an elephant. After that, the animal must be returned to Laos. That alone would not be illegal. However, it cannot be verified whether some elephants from Wild Elephant Valley were returned to Laos after their lease expired two years ago.

The Wild Elephant Valley press office declined to comment on the matter and recommended contacting the local tourism authority. They also declined to comment and suggested contacting the media office of the Xishuangbanna municipal government. ## Zurich Zoo receives animals free of charge

But how can a zoo legally acquire an elephant without leasing it? We asked Severin Dressen, director of Zurich Zoo, for more information.

![Severin Dressen, Director of the Zurich Zoo.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/07/31/820f8db9-5292-4aa2-8c0d-c4dc03042a72.jpg?width=1024)
*Severin Dressen, Director of the Zurich Zoo.* (Keystone)

«Because Asian elephants are critically endangered, we are participating in a breeding program as a scientifically managed zoo to help preserve the species,» says Dressen.

Here's how it works: In Europe, all scientifically managed zoos are members of the European Association of Zoos and Aquaria, or EAZA, which runs over 500 breeding programs . Member zoos participate in these breeding programs and exchange animals among themselves. A prerequisite for membership of EAZA and its breeding programs is that zoos engage in education, conservation, research, and species protection.

«In principle, all animals within the EAZA are exchanged free of charge, even those that are not currently part of a European breeding program, that's standard,» says Dressen. When a zoo receives a new animal, it only covers the logistics costs. Only a few animal species, such as some marine fish species, would be purchased by Zurich Zoo. However, the zoo is currently reorganizing its animal population so that such a purchase will no longer be necessary in the future.

![Elephants at Zurich Zoo with a newborn calf in 2017.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/07/30/bffccc87-ba4b-4f81-ae48-1cb44b4d7fd3.jpg?crop=4949,3299,x343,y413)
*Elephants at Zurich Zoo with a newborn calf in 2017.* (Ennio Leanza / Keystone)

Chinese zoos have also established an umbrella organization, the China Zoo Association. However, Chinese zoos lag far behind EAZA standards when it comes to the exchange of animals as part of breeding programs. Breeding programs for native species such as giant pandas, golden monkeys, and Delacour's langurs are exceptions to this rule.

Animal welfare has not always been a priority in Switzerland either. In the 1990s, Swiss circuses staged controversial elephant shows. To date, it is still permitted to keep wild animals in circuses in Switzerland. This has long been prohibited in other European countries. And in China, it is still a lucrative business.
