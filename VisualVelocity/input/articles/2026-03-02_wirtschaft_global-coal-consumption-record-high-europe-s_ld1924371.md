# Global coal consumption is at a record high. Europe's focus on reducing its own CO2 emissions has failed.

*Unilateral commitments have proven ineffective in the fight against climate change. Economist Axel Ockenfels explains why selfishness is more promising than altruism.*

**Michael Rasch** · Wirtschaft · 2026-03-02 · 1546 words · [ld.1924371](https://www.nzz.ch/wirtschaft/der-globale-kohleverbrauch-ist-auf-einem-rekordhoch-der-europaeische-fokus-auf-die-reduktion-des-eigenen-co2-ausstosses-hat-versagt-ld.1924371)

Germany's phase-out of coal mining and combustion for electricity generation and industrial use is underway. The towns of Grevenbroich in North Rhine-Westphalia , Hohenhameln in Lower Saxony, Jänschwalde in Brandenburg, and Zolling in Bavaria all have one thing in common: a coal-fired power plant was shut down in each of them in recent years. The list could be considerably longer.

While coal use has long played a negligible role in Switzerland, Germany, a major consumer, has legally mandated a binding timetable: all coal-fired power plants are to be shut down by 2038. The operators will receive billions in compensation, financed by taxpayers. However, this unilateral German and European coal phase-out has virtually no impact on the climate. Globally, coal combustion reached yet another record high in 2025 – and this level is expected to remain high for the foreseeable future.

## Failed voluntary commitments

«Coal is a major problem; we have to end its use,» Axel Ockenfels said in an interview. «The question, however, is: how?» added the economics professor at the University of Cologne and director at the Max Planck Institute for Behavioral Economics in Bonn.

For Ockenfels, the fight against climate change is primarily a problem of cooperation. If a single country like Germany or a single region like the EU were to take ambitious climate measures, the full costs in the form of higher energy prices and competitive disadvantages would be borne directly and very concentrated there, while the climate benefits would be small and distributed equally among all countries – including those that do not make an effort.

«Climate change can only be solved collectively. But with the Paris Climate Agreement, which relies on voluntary commitments, we have been on the wrong track for eleven years,» says Ockenfels. He knows of no example where voluntary commitments have solved an international cooperation problem.

The economist points to similar challenges. «Hardly anyone would consider unilaterally disarming or unilaterally lowering tariffs, hoping that the other side would follow suit,» Ockenfels explains. «The idea that enough others would follow our moral example against their own interests contradicts cooperation research.» This approach, he notes, is only attempted in the reduction of climate-damaging greenhouse gases.

The global coal dilemma remains unresolved: coal is the largest source of climate-damaging CO2 emissions, yet in many countries it is also a mainstay of electricity production. This is unlikely to change anytime soon, as the world's demand for ever more electricity continues.

Burning black rock is even more harmful to the climate than burning oil or natural gas, yet many countries cannot meet their energy needs without it. Despite all efforts, the expansion of renewable energies is either too slow or too expensive.

Last year, global coal consumption reached a record high of 8,845 million tons, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA), representing a 0.5 percent increase compared to 2024. Coal production remained at the previous year's level, but at 9,111 million tons, it was even higher. The IEA anticipates only a slight decline of around 5 percent in both consumption and production by 2030. Coal will therefore remain highly relevant for energy supply for a long time to come – regardless of whether Europe phases out coal or not.

According to the «Production Gap Report 2025», governments are now planning even higher coal production by 2035 than in 2023. The planned increases in fossil fuel production would lead to global production levels in 2030 that are 500 percent (coal), 31 percent (oil) and 92 percent (gas) above the median value of the scenario agreed upon with the Paris 1.5-degree target.

The carbon leakage problem and the waterbed effect play a significant role in the failure of a unilateral European phase-out. The former describes the phenomenon that when one country abandons fossil fuels, global prices for oil, gas, and coal fall, making their use more attractive to others. Alternatively, energy-intensive companies simply relocate to regions with less ambitious climate targets. In this case, climate altruists effectively subsidize the CO2 emissions of those who prioritize self-interest.

The «waterbed effect» describes a similar phenomenon in the European emissions trading system, where local CO2 savings, for example through the coal phase-out in Germany, do not lead to an overall reduction in emissions. Since the total number of certificates is limited, fewer emissions in one location result in cheaper certificates in another. Therefore, focusing solely on one's own CO2 footprint is insufficient. At least the EU has now begun to address this problem.

## Leakage effect undermines incentives for cooperation

«While a unilateral phase-out can serve a national climate goal, it doesn’t necessarily bring us any closer to saving the climate ,» says Ockenfels. On the contrary, leakage and waterbed effects could even motivate other countries to consume more fossil fuels. The expert on cooperation issues calls for a focus on strategies that incentivize others to participate. The question is how to achieve a collective agreement without large emitting countries acting as free riders.

«On the one hand, technological innovations can help reduce the costs of green energy and thus the costs of international climate cooperation,» says Ockenfels. «But cooperation also requires reciprocity, because that protects those willing to participate from free riders and at the same time motivates those unwilling to do so.»

«Reciprocity can take many forms, but it seems advisable to start with a smaller group of willing countries that are politically and economically strong,» the economist adds. One approach would be to agree with these countries on a minimum CO2 price, which might initially be lower than what Europe would prefer. However, this would increase the chances of successfully embarking on genuine cooperation.

Then measures are needed to incentivize reluctant participants to join in. This could, for example, involve taxing their imports at the level of the agreed-upon minimum CO2 price. The European Union (EU) is pursuing this approach with its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM). It aims to create a level playing field by subjecting imports from countries with weaker climate regulations to similar CO2 costs as goods produced within the EU.

Brussels aims to prevent counterproductive effects such as carbon leakage and at the same time create incentives for other countries to price CO2 themselves, because this allows other countries to avoid handing over their CO2 revenues to Europe.

New ideas are needed. On the one hand, there has been progress in the fight against the «coal crisis,» as some call the persistently high consumption – but there are also setbacks. On the one hand, according to the IEA, the strong expansion of renewable energies is helping to dampen demand. Furthermore, the increasing export capacity for liquefied natural gas (LNG), thanks to falling prices, could encourage many countries to replace coal consumption with the less harmful gas, it is said.

On the other hand, rising global electricity demand is likely to further fuel coal consumption, especially since two-thirds of global coal use is currently for electricity generation. Political changes, for example in the U.S., could also lead to a coal revival. However, key factors influencing demand include not only political measures, but also the prices of alternative energy sources and weather conditions.

Developments in China are crucial for the fight against coal. The country produces and consumes more coal than the rest of the world combined. In the Middle Kingdom, the authorities are not only massively expanding renewable energies, but also coal-fired power generation. Chinese dominance means that the global coal market is heavily dependent on events there.

A similar situation exists in India, by far the second-largest producer and consumer of coal. However, supply and demand on the subcontinent reach only a quarter of the Chinese figures. While an early and heavy monsoon season in India last year led to lower electricity demand, boosting hydropower production, the IEA expects Indian coal consumption to increase by a substantial 17 percent by 2030.

Special effects on annual consumption are not uncommon, but long-term trends are crucial. In the US, for example, according to the IEA, coal demand fell by an average of 6 percent annually over the past 15 years. Then, in 2025, it rose again by 8 percent. This was due to a combination of higher natural gas prices and a slowdown in the shutdown of coal-fired power plants, a measure implemented by the administration of President Donald Trump.

## Europe is sticking to its complete coal phase-out

And what about Europe? The old continent has so far continued working towards a complete phase-out of coal. Six countries have already completed this, including Belgium, Austria, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. A further eight countries, including France, Denmark, and Greece, aim to phase it out by 2030.

Germany and six other countries are aiming for this in the future. The strong expansion of renewable energies, the use of nuclear power, and rising costs for CO2 emissions due to the emissions trading system support this development, with Germany, under the leadership of the Green Party, choosing to phase out nuclear energy first rather than climate-damaging coal.

Ockenfels believes the European phase-out of coal is fundamentally the right approach, but the method is wrong. «Egoism is more contagious than altruism,» says the economist. «Focusing on one's own carbon footprint doesn't solve the cooperation problem. Innovation and cooperation can make a real difference, but they require a comprehensive reorientation of climate policy.»
