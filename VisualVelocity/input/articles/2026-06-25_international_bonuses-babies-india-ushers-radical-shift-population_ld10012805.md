# Bonuses for babies: India ushers in a radical shift in population policy

*For decades, India combated its population explosion with sterilizations and family planning. But in recent years, the birth rate has plummeted. Now, Indian politicians want to counteract this trend. Critics warn of undesirable consequences.*

The Indian state of Andhra Pradesh has recently begun rewarding large families with financial assistance. In May, Chief Minister Chandrababu Naidu announced that families will receive 30,000 rupees for their third child and 40,000 rupees for their fourth. This is roughly equivalent to 260 to 340 Swiss francs. This is a substantial sum by Indian standards, representing 12% and 16%, respectively, of an average annual income.

With over 1.4 billion inhabitants, India has been the world's most populous country since 2023. Nevertheless, demographic anxiety is spreading there. The birth rate has declined sharply in recent years and, at 1.9 children per woman, is now below the 2.1 needed to maintain the population size. And the decline has not yet bottomed out. Some demographers predict an ultra-low birth rate of approximately 1.3 children for India by the middle of the century.

Nationalists have long been concerned about the declining birth rate. Mohan Bhagwat, head of the Hindu nationalist RSS, warned in 2022 that societies unable to sustain themselves were threatened with extinction, adding: «Many languages and cultures have already disappeared because of this problem. It is therefore essential to maintain a birth rate above 2.1.» Hindu nationalists advocate that married couples have at least three children.

## Southern India has less to say

It is no longer just nationalists who are warning of demographic trends like those already seen in Western and East Asian societies. Furthermore, a north-south demographic divide is spreading across the country, altering the balance of power.

While the birth rate in the northern states still hovers between two and three children, it is already far lower in the wealthier south of the country. Tamil Nadu has the lowest rate at 1.3 children. As a consequence, it faces the prospect of reduced state funding from Delhi and fewer seats in parliament. It is therefore no coincidence that the southern state of Andhra Pradesh (birth rate 1.5 children) is now taking the lead in promoting births. Other states are likely to follow suit.

## From forced sterilization to birth promotion

This development stands in stark contrast to earlier policies driven by fears of overpopulation. Since the 1960s, India had therefore focused on contraception and family planning. At that time, the birth rate was just under six children per woman, and the population was 450 million.

During Indira Gandhi's government in the 1970s, a particularly drastic measure was taken: millions of Indians were sterilized against their will, most of them poor men. Today, however, it is primarily mothers of multiple children who undergo sterilization in state-funded clinics and receive sterilization bonuses for doing so.

But now a pronatalist shift is emerging in India. This is drawing criticism from left-wing and feminist circles. Poonam Muttreja, who has been advocating for mothers' rights for several decades and heads the NGO Population Foundation of India, warns against forced pregnancies. Instead, she argues for a better work-life balance and immigration from states with high birth rates: «Women's desires have changed. Young women today no longer want to go through the same hardship as our grandmothers,» she writes in an article for the Indian newspaper «South First.»

## More senior citizens, fewer children

Meanwhile, the demographic decline is difficult to halt because the number of young people in India is already decreasing. India had its highest number of children and young people at the end of the 2000s. At the same time, the number of elderly people is rising dramatically. According to U.N. data, in 2024, for the first time, over 100 million Indians were 65 years of age or older. 20 years earlier, the figure was only about half that. This aging population poses particular challenges for India compared to Western societies, because despite impressive growth rates, India remains a predominantly poor country.

In the perception of most Indians, this reality is likely still a long way off. India is one of the most densely populated countries in the world, and the U.N. predicts a population increase to 1.7 billion by 2060 before it begins to decline again. However, whether this will actually happen is by no means certain. In the past, demographers' forecasts have repeatedly proven to be overly optimistic.
