# 14 billion for football players: The transfer market is booming like never before. But a court ruling calls the system into question

*Never before have football clubs spent as much money on players as this year. A renowned sports law expert warns the generous clubs: they've only bought hot air. The reason is the verdict in the Diarra case.*

On Monday, at the end of the summer transfer window, the market heated up once again. Alexander Isak, the Swedish striker, moved to Liverpool FC for almost 150 million euros.

Isak's transfer was the fitting end to a remarkable transfer year in which over €14 billion changed hands worldwide, combining the winter and summer transfer windows. According to an analysis by the CIES Football Observatory, this is 14 percent more than the previous record year of 2023, setting a new, unparalleled record.

A new year, a new record. Business as usual in the football industry, which has long since left the difficult years of the pandemic behind. The billions are flowing again like a mountain stream in spring when the snow melts at higher altitudes. In this case, the Premier League is the source , having secured increasingly lucrative television contracts that allow it to treat its competitors like mere suppliers.

So everything remains the same, or rather: everything is bigger than ever before. Meanwhile, the current transfer system is under attack and its future is at stake. And there are legal experts who say it will soon undergo fundamental changes.

## The class action lawsuits against FIFA

Lassana Diarra was barely mentioned during the recent, hectic transfer months. The Frenchman was ignored like an uninvited guest who somehow managed to sneak in. But perhaps his name will one day go down in football history like John Bosman's , because he forced something upon the football ecosystem that it dislikes intensely: change.

Diarra only briefly made the headlines. In August, his lawyers announced that the former French professional footballer was demanding €65 million in damages from FIFA. Just a few days earlier, the organization «Justice for Players» had announced that it was launching a class-action lawsuit against FIFA, the German Football Association (DFB), and other European national associations.

The announcements are the result of a ruling by the European Court of Justice last October. And they represent a power struggle between players' unions and the football establishment that will keep the industry on edge for some time to come.

Diarra had a respectable career, dominating midfield for Real Madrid and earning 34 caps for France. In 2014, he was under contract with Lokomotiv Moscow. And then he became embroiled in a dispute that continues to this day.

The Frenchman unilaterally terminated his contract in Moscow and was later fined €10 million by FIFA. This set in motion a chain of events that ultimately led the European Court of Justice to question the very foundations of FIFA's international transfer system. FIFA's regulations stipulate that players and their new clubs must pay compensation if they terminate a contract prematurely without a valid reason. Furthermore, the system provides for additional sanctions in such cases, including bans on the players and transfer restrictions for the acquiring clubs.

The European Court of Justice ruled that parts of the FIFA regulations violated EU law and that, in their current form, they primarily served to protect the financial interests of clubs. According to long-time observers, the court expressed itself with exceptional clarity in its ruling.

Antoine Duval conducts research at the Asser Institute in the Netherlands and is a renowned sports law expert. The Frenchman is among those experts who have offered the most pointed commentary on the Diarra ruling. And he is convinced that it will have a «transformative character.»

Duval says that the transfer market only exists in its current form because FIFA rules create risks for players and clubs – the risk of having to pay high compensation or being banned from playing or transferring. «Clubs and players want to avoid these risks, which is why they seek an amicable agreement, and that's why transfer fees are paid,» Duval said.

Since the European Court of Justice ruling in the Diarra case, these risks no longer exist in the same form for Duval. «Strictly speaking, from a legal point of view, in my opinion the clubs have spent billions on hot air since the ruling, on a pipe dream, and have thus taken on great risks,» he says.

The reason: If a footballer were to unilaterally terminate his contract now, according to Duval, it would be «highly unlikely» that FIFA would impose the same sanctions and compensation payments as in the past, due to the EU ruling. This is because FIFA would risk further damage claims, in addition to those already threatened by Diarra's lawsuit and that of «Justice for Players.»

## Jean-Louis Dupont, Bosman's lawyer, is involved again

The Dutch organization aims to encourage footballers to join the class-action lawsuit. It argues that up to 100,000 players have suffered income losses due to the unlawful FIFA regulations. «Justice for Players» is being advised by a familiar face, Jean-Louis Dupont. He secured the Bosman ruling, becoming a thorn in the side of the football industry. His law firm is now also representing Diarra.

Dupont's partner, Martin Hissel, Diarra's lawyer, is surprised that last year's ruling has not yet influenced players' behavior. Hissel emphasizes the differences to the Bosman ruling. Back then, it was clear that footballers could transfer for free after their contracts expired. Now, the issue concerns ongoing employment relationships, which means players must take action, terminate their contracts – and thus incur legal risks.

Legally, FIFA's transfer system has been on shaky ground since the Diarra ruling. Behind the scenes, players' unions are struggling with FIFA and other stakeholders, such as the European Club Association (ECA), to establish a new set of rules; the compensation claims must be viewed in this context. The players' union FIFPro envisions a system that more clearly defines when and at what price a footballer can terminate his contract. This is intended to put an end to the price gouging in the transfer market.

FIFA and the clubs are playing for time. The world governing body only stated upon inquiry that discussions regarding future transfer rules are ongoing. Since the ruling, FIFA and the clubs have maintained that while adjustments are necessary due to the Diarra verdict, the system can largely continue as before. This is also because the well-oiled transfer machine reliably distributes the money. If it were to stop running, there would be many losers. The only question is how long it will continue to do so.
