# Air Conditioners Have a Bad Reputation – Yet They Are Being Bought in Droves

*Environmentally harmful refrigerants, high energy consumption, and additional heat in the streets: cool air comes at a price. But choosing the right device can make a big difference.*

Scorching 38 degrees during the day, tropical 23 degrees at night: The June heatwave pushed people in Europe to their limits. Many probably made the decision during those sweaty nights: It's time for an air conditioner.

But those who buy one are often plagued by their conscience, because air conditioners are considered harmful to the environment and the climate.

But what exactly is the problem with these devices, and are modern systems really that bad?

Harmful or not: Demand is rising

Air conditioners come in two types: Some are permanently installed in buildings. To install one in Switzerland, a building permit is required, which is rarely granted for residential buildings. These devices are also rare in Germany; even in new builds, only one in twenty residential buildings has an air conditioner installed.

But there are also small, mobile air conditioning units. Anyone can set these up in their own home; no permit is needed, and they are easy to install. These systems are becoming increasingly popular. According to online retailer Galaxus, it sold more of them in June than ever before.

Accordingly, costs skyrocketed in June. The average sales price of an air conditioner this year is 452 Swiss francs, significantly higher than before. An NZZ analysis of Galaxus prices also shows that prices rose sharply at the beginning of the heatwave. Fans, however, are no more expensive than a year ago.

The refrigerant

The refrigerant is considered a particular environmental pollutant. It flows inside the air conditioner in a closed circuit. It should never leave the device and thus should not be able to cause environmental damage. But: «If a leak occurs and the refrigerant escapes, it becomes a problem,» says Joachim Seifert. He is a professor at the Institute for Energy Technology at the Technical University of Dresden and conducts research on efficient heating and cooling systems, among other topics.

Even the smallest cracks in the cooling system can be enough for gas to escape. In addition, the units are often not disposed of properly, and the refrigerant ends up in the air, at the latest when the air conditioner is in a landfill.

But not every refrigerant is equally harmful. Especially in old systems, fluorinated gases are often used. These usually have a high greenhouse effect; some have a warming effect in the atmosphere many hundreds of times greater than carbon dioxide. In addition, certain refrigerants decompose in the atmosphere into toxic substances such as TFA. This chemical can cause infertility and is difficult to filter out or break down. Today, TFA is found practically everywhere in groundwater in Switzerland.

> [embed: datawrapper chart ]

However, fluorinated gases are mainly used in car air conditioners today. Most modern home units run on a significantly more environmentally friendly alternative: propane, also abbreviated as refrigerant R290. Its climate impact is manageable. However, the gas is highly flammable; it is used, for example, as fuel for camping stoves. Since the gas is heavier than air, it would accumulate on the ground in the event of a leak. Burning candles and other sources of fire should therefore not be placed next to the air conditioner.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 833c3c20b50add8a4b6f953007c51e5b]

So, if you set up a propane-cooled unit, you don't have to worry much about the environment because of the refrigerant. Electricity consumption weighs more heavily.

Electricity consumption

Air conditioners consume a lot of electricity, and the popular mobile monoblock units, in particular, are especially inefficient. With these, the entire air conditioner is in the room, and only a hose is hung out the window. Mobile split units, on the other hand, are more similar to permanently installed devices and have a part that is placed outside on the balcony or hung on the window. Since they do not require drilling and can be quickly dismantled, no permit is needed for these devices either.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 85a7e70188cdb319eb095476128cf5a0]

Monoblock units blow some of the air they have drawn from the room out the window. This creates a negative pressure in the room. This ensures that warm air from outside flows back in through every crack, partially nullifying the cooling effect. It is even more inefficient if the window from which the hose hangs is poorly sealed – or if the hose does not lead outside at all. In such cases, the device can run at full power, yet the room can hardly cool down.

Split units bypass this problem by drawing in the air for cooling the refrigerant directly from outside. This prevents negative pressure in the room.

While running at full load, a mobile air conditioner typically consumes around 1,000 watts. In 24 hours, that is about as much electricity as an electric car consumes over 100 kilometers. How harmful this electricity consumption is for the climate primarily depends on the country's energy mix.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 833c3c20b50add8a4b6f9530078b3d8f]

Solar energy, in particular, could fundamentally be well utilized for operating air conditioners, as a particularly large amount of it is generated in midsummer. On sunny summer days, Switzerland produces more electricity than it needs or can export, and in Germany, there are sometimes negative electricity prices on summer weekends.

However, the cheapest electricity is available during the day – but most people in private homes need cooling in the evenings and at night. In addition, if the solar system is not on one's own roof, the power grid has to transport large amounts of electricity to consumers. This further strains the power grid, which is already under stress during heatwaves.

Air conditioners not only strain the power grid but possibly also the neighbors. For them to function, they must blow hot air outside. So the concern is obvious: Do they heat up cities even more?

The additional heat

Researchers at Humboldt University in Berlin tried to answer this question, at least for Berlin. In a scientific study, they describe several simulations on how much air conditioners could heat up the city. The result: If air conditioners were used everywhere, they could measurably increase temperatures in the city. However, only by about half a degree. Older studies on the heat effect of air conditioners in Tokyo or Paris sometimes found warming of up to 2 degrees.

The effect is thus significantly weaker than the urban heat island effect, which arises from dense development with few green spaces. Because houses and road surfaces heat up strongly during the day, the wind hardly blows cool air through the alleys and few trees lower the temperature through evaporation, it is up to seven degrees warmer at night in the city center than in the agglomeration.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 25adbb1c7f644d6e4d79c92af9ea55ea]

Refrigerants, electricity consumption and heat in the streets: More mobile air conditioners certainly bring problems. Nevertheless, Joachim Seifert does not consider it right to demonize them generally. «With a modern device that is properly installed, you are not doing anything inherently bad for the environment,» he says.

However, anyone who wants to be cooler for the rest of the summer might have a hard time. At least at online retailer Galaxus, many devices are already sold out. Those who order now often cannot expect delivery for several weeks. For some devices, replenishment could even be delayed until after the end of the summer season.
