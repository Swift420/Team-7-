# Who has the right to decide over life and death? An overview of euthanasia in Europe

*Assisted dying raises one of life's most difficult ethical questions. Few political debates are more emotionally charged, as the recent decision in France demonstrates. The legal situation in Europe is correspondingly diverse.*

In 2024, a young Spanish woman applied for euthanasia. She suffered from borderline personality disorder. Following a suicide attempt in 2022, she was paraplegic and had severe chronic pain. 19 experts, including doctors, lawyers and ethicists, certified that the woman had an «incurable clinical condition» and approved her application.

Euthanasia has been legal in Spain since 2021. However, because the father and a Catholic lobbying group took legal action against the decision, it took 700 days before doctors could administer a lethal cocktail of drugs to the young woman.

The case sparked intense and emotional debate across Europe. It is symbolic of the complexity of the moral and legal questions surrounding euthanasia. Is there a right to a self-determined death? Who should decide on life and death? Is it morally justifiable to help someone commit suicide whose illness, while incurable, is not fatal?

Various European countries believe they have found a legal answer to these questions.

## Few countries have legalized active euthanasia

Morality and law distinguish between active euthanasia, assisted suicide, and passive euthanasia. The fundamental question is: How much external intervention in the dying process is permissible or even desirable? In active euthanasia, the patient is given a lethal drug. In assisted suicide, the patient receives the drug but must take it themselves. Passive euthanasia means foregoing life-prolonging measures. This usually requires an advance directive.

In mid-July, France's parliament approved a law legalizing euthanasia under numerous conditions. The patient must be incurably ill, in an advanced stage, and suffering unbearably. They must still be able to freely and clearly express their wish for euthanasia and take the lethal substance themselves. If they are physically unable to do so, a doctor or nurse will administer it.

The list of conditions goes on, as an agreement was not possible otherwise. And yet, with this law, France goes further than most European states.

In Europe, active euthanasia is legal not only in Spain but also in Luxembourg. In the Netherlands and Belgium, the liberal legislation also applies to children. In all these countries, the person must be incurably ill and suffering greatly. Portugal also passed a law legalizing active euthanasia in 2023, but its practical implementation has so far failed.

Active euthanasia is prohibited in Switzerland, but assisted suicide is permitted. The conditions are not defined by law, but rather by medical expert panels and in the statutes of assisted suicide organizations. This practice has been established for decades, which is why people from abroad also travel to Switzerland for assisted suicide.

In Germany, the Federal Constitutional Court overturned the ban on assisted suicide in 2020. The court ruled that the general right of personality includes the right to a self-determined death, which also includes the right to seek assistance from others. However, assisted suicide is still not legally regulated; two drafts failed in the Bundestag in 2023. In Austria, by contrast, passive euthanasia has been permitted since 2022.

Despite at least partial legalization in these countries, euthanasia is subject to strict conditions. In individual cases, it can take a long time for euthanasia to be granted. Sometimes the person is already dead by then. This makes the debates particularly emotional: In individual cases, what the public considers morally right can differ significantly from the legal regulations. A single article of law cannot always do justice to this complexity.

Switzerland is therefore considered an example of a pragmatic approach to assisted suicide: There is no single, universally applicable law, but rather rules that have developed over the years. Not only the penal code applies, but also ethical guidelines from the medical field. Consequently, Switzerland never experienced a protracted, emotionally charged debate like the one in France.

## Religious resistance

In most European countries, euthanasia is either not regulated by law or explicitly prohibited. Euthanasia is a criminal offense in Denmark, Greece, Ireland, Norway, Poland, Scotland, Finland, Sweden and the Czech Republic, among others. In Hungary, only passive euthanasia is permitted, meaning forgoing life-sustaining measures.

In Italy, euthanasia is illegal, but not punishable in exceptional cases. This stems from a 2019 ruling by the Constitutional Court. The patient must be incurably ill, dependent on life-sustaining measures, suffering unbearable physical and psychological pain, but still fully capable of making free decisions and understanding their consequences. In November 2021, a man was permitted to receive active euthanasia for the first time. An ethics committee took more than a year to reach its decision.

In 2025, a decision in Tuscany caused a stir. It became the first region in Italy to pass its own law permitting medically assisted suicide.

In late 2024, the House of Commons approved a reform proposal to legalize assisted dying. However, the House of Lords, which includes 26 bishops, has tabled countless amendments. The reform could now fail due to the legislative deadline.

In France, assisted dying also remains a criminal offense. The law has not yet come into effect. At the request of Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, the law is still being reviewed by the Constitutional Council.
