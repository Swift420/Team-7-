# Not keen on military service? Europe's defense has a recruitment problem. The big picture.

*European countries are debating the reintroduction of conscription. Where does it apply? Where doesn't it? And what do Europe's youth think?*

**Julia Monn** · International · 2025-04-02 · 1345 words · [ld.1876768](https://www.nzz.ch/international/wehrpflicht-in-europa-zahlen-fakten-und-aktuelle-umfragen-im-ueberblick-ld.1876768)

**

Europe plans to prepare for a more uncertain world by investing billions in new tanks, aircraft, and missile defense systems. But while the debate rages over where all this money will come from, the next, and potentially even bigger, problem is already emerging: Who will operate all this equipment? There is a shortage of soldiers, and a significant one at that, say experts, including Cameron Abadi in an interview with the NZZ.

## How many soldiers does Europe have, and where is military service compulsory?

If war were to break out today, the more than thirty countries of Europe could together mobilize just under two million active soldiers. That is only slightly more than the USA alone can provide. This refers to professional soldiers who would be ready for immediate deployment. Additional forces, i.e., reservists, would first have to be mobilized and equipped.

> [embed: datawrapper chart ]

It is striking that among the ten countries with the most active soldiers, only Turkey and Greece rely on conscription. Both countries are also using military means to wage their dispute over islands and resources in the eastern Mediterranean.

Otherwise, conscription is maintained primarily in countries bordering Russia—such as those in Scandinavia and the Baltic states—or in the neutral states of Switzerland and Austria. In the Balkans , Croatia and Serbia are reintroducing conscription this year. Furthermore, Denmark, as the second European country after Norway, is extending compulsory military service to women.

However, the length of service, the age at which soldiers are drafted, and whether there are alternatives such as civilian service are regulated differently everywhere.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 345456dee30e23ccd832188381b3195b]

Here are three examples: In Turkey, men between the ages of 20 and 41 are required to perform 6 to 12 months of military service. However, it is possible to pay a fee to be exempt from this service . Furthermore, those who are studying or work in certain professions in research, the arts, or elite sports are allowed to postpone their service.

Finland has a different system. Men aged 18 and over complete 5.5 to 12 months of military, border guard, or civilian service. After that, they belong to the reserves and can be called up in an emergency until the age of 50, or until the age of 60 for non-commissioned officers or officers.

Switzerland also pursues its own approach with its militia system. Every Swiss citizen is obligated to perform military service or civilian service. The majority complete their service over several years following a short basic training course, with refresher courses. Military service lasts at least 245 days, civilian service 368 days. Service is generally completed between the ages of 18 and 30. Afterward, one belongs to the reserves; the length of service depends on rank.

## How have troop strengths changed?

Despite different models, European countries with conscription have one thing in common: they have thousands of soldiers in reserve who must be ready for deployment in case of emergency.

> [embed: datawrapper chart ]

Over the past decade, many countries have significantly reduced their armed forces – and not only in countries with conscription. Large all-volunteer armies like those of Great Britain, France, and Italy also saw substantial personnel reductions between 2015 and 2025. In Germany and Spain, troop strength increased only minimally. And the situation is no better with reserves: even in large NATO countries, they remain manageable, often numbering only a few tens of thousands of soldiers.

> [embed: datawrapper chart ]

The abolition of conscription marked the beginning of the decline in army numbers. Great Britain started the process in 1960, followed by France in 1997, Spain in 2001, and Italy in 2005. Germany was the last to take this step, suspending conscription there only in 2011.

The restructuring of the armed forces was the result of shifts in security and socio-political contexts. With the end of the Cold War, traditional national defense and conventional warfare lost importance. They were replaced by deployments abroad in crisis regions and disaster relief. Full-time soldiers were considered better suited for these tasks than conscripts.

Governments and experts assumed that professional armies were not only more efficient in handling increasingly complex weapons systems, but also cheaper than massive mass armies. This was because the latter were expensive not only in terms of recruitment, but also in terms of housing, equipment, and training. At the same time, the willingness to perform military service declined in many societies. Military service was considered outdated, whether for pacifist reasons or because of a lack of financial and social incentives.

The Russian war of aggression against Ukraine has changed the security landscape since 2022. Yet only one large all-volunteer army has responded: Poland . Since the outbreak of the war in Ukraine alone, its armed forces have grown by approximately 50,000 soldiers and are set to expand further. While the Polish government does not intend to reinstate conscription, it is planning voluntary military training for all adult men to ensure a broader troop pool in case of emergency.

## What does the population think about a reintroduction?

Poland's continued reliance on voluntary military service, despite its direct proximity to the war in Ukraine, is drawing criticism within the country itself. Conservative politicians and high-ranking military officials are calling for a return to conscription. They believe the population supports them. According to recent polls, 55 percent of the population favors the reintroduction of military service.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 50f5f9f48c3ba32070a3a08cc65fa8b5]

However, there is a clear difference between the generations: While the 18- to 29-year-olds – i.e., potential recruits – are mostly against conscription, those over 30 are clearly in favor of it.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 50f5f9f48c3ba32070a3a08cc6410660]

Even the idea of ​​voluntary military training divides the generations in Poland. While a majority of the population supports it, many under 30 reject the idea. Nevertheless, Poland's compromise between volunteering and conscription is likely to be closely watched in Western European capitals. From Paris to Berlin to Madrid, the question of mandatory service is once again resurfacing in public debate.

However, only in France and Germany is there currently a clear majority of the population in favor of reintroducing compulsory military service. The situation is different in Italy, Great Britain, and Spain, where a majority opposes its reintroduction. This is shown by a survey conducted in March by the YouGov polling institute for these countries.

> [embed: datawrapper chart ]

The results also reflect other national surveys. However, as in Poland, it shows that even in France and Germany, young people have little support for compulsory military service. It is the 35- to 40-year-olds who overwhelmingly favor it.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) ff42fc9dffbfb98a85c0a482801befae]

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) ff42fc9dffbfb98a85c0a48280674f61]

In Italy and Spain, even among older generations, there is a lack of clear support for conscription. However, support is lowest among potential recruits, where only about a third would be in favor of mandatory service.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 50f5f9f48c3ba32070a3a08cc60fbbd9]

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 50f5f9f48c3ba32070a3a08cc641a983]

The situation is quite different in Switzerland, where conscription is still in place. According to the latest study by the army, only 31 percent of those surveyed want to abolish the system. Among 18- to 34-year-olds, the figure is higher at 39 percent – ​​but this represents a significant decline: in 2023, almost half of young people were in favor of ending conscription.

## How high is the willingness to fight in an emergency?

While the debate about military service is often conducted theoretically, in a real emergency a completely different question comes to the fore: Who would actually be willing to fight for their country in a crisis?

A year ago, a survey in Great Britain caused a stir in this context. It showed that not only do many Britons oppose conscription; around a third of those under 40 even stated that they would refuse military service if their country were threatened with invasion.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 50f5f9f48c3ba32070a3a08cc65962d1]

The willingness to fight for one's country in a crisis is, in recent times, about as moderate among the British as in other major European countries. According to a Gallup poll conducted every ten years, in 2024 less than half of respondents in most European countries would take up arms to defend their country in a crisis.

In Great Britain and Spain, around a third of the population would still fight. In Germany and Austria, this figure drops to 20 percent. Italy brings up the rear: only 14 percent of the population would be prepared to defend the country in a crisis. Compared to the 2014 survey, this willingness has declined even further.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) 50f5f9f48c3ba32070a3a08cc63f89f2]

The situation is different in Finland. Ten years ago, two-thirds of Finns declared their willingness to fight for their country in a crisis – even though Russia had not yet attacked Ukraine and Finland was not yet a NATO member. Both of these things have changed since then.
