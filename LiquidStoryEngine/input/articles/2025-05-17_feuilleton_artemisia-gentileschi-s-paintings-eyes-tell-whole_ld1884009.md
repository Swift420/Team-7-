# In Artemisia Gentileschi's paintings, the eyes tell the whole story

*The Rome-born painter is one of the most important artists of the Baroque period. A comprehensive exhibition of her work is now on display in Paris, including previously unseen paintings.*

No one painted eyes like hers. They offer a glimpse into the psyche, reflecting the highest drama. While Artemisia Gentileschi painted emblematic stories from the Bible, as was then considered the canon of visual art, she achieved what many at the time did not dare: she brought forth individuals, her biblical stories depicting scenes that resonate with everyday life.

Among these works is the spectacular painting «Susanna and the Elders» from 1610. She was 17 years old when she painted it. It took courage to depict the woman's nudity in the bath so explicitly—all the more so since Gentileschi likely used himself as a model in the mirror—and the fear she conveys in the young woman's face is also fundamental; disgust and despair scream from her eyes. These are the antithesis of the lust of the two elderly people who assail her from beyond the wall.

«Susanna and the Elders» is the centerpiece of the major exhibition currently on display at the Musée Jacquemart-André in Paris. It was the first painting that Artemisia Gentileschi, born in Rome in 1593, signed with her full name. Her name was practically carved in stone beneath the painting: as an inscription in the brickwork on which Susanna sits bathing.

One could call the painting prophetic, but Gentileschi likely only depicted what women of his time could expect, based on the biblical account of Susanna's slander. Less than a year later, Artemisia Gentileschi was raped by a painter in her father's and teacher's workshop. Only the promise that he would soon marry her prevented her from stabbing the perpetrator. When he later broke his promise, Artemisia's father ensured that he was tried and exiled.

## The young Artemisia paints a masterpiece

Susanna at her Bath is a popular subject in painting of the time and frequently requested by patrons. It is presumed that Artemisia's father, Orazio, suggested the motif to her. It was intended to be her journeyman's piece, with which he planned to introduce her to the art world.

Radiological examinations have revealed that the preliminary drawing most likely originated with the father, while Artemisia was responsible for the painting itself. The authorship, prominently attested to by her signature, is the exclamation mark with which the artist is released into independence from her father. Of course, all of this would have been for naught if the painting had not demonstrated the artist's extraordinary talent and if the young woman had not already practiced her art with great mastery.

Two years later, Orazio wrote a letter of recommendation to Christine of Lorraine, the wife of Ferdinando I de' Medici. It seems to support the assumption that the father had long suspected how far his daughter would surpass him: «Besides three sons, I also have a daughter, and this daughter, as was God's will, I trained as a painter in three years, so that today she is so skilled that I dare say she is unparalleled (...), as I will show Your Highness in due time and place.»

Artemisia Gentileschi painted the Susanna motif several more times later, but no version achieved the expressiveness and directness with which the viewer was drawn in as a witness to the assault, nor the urgency of the 1610 painting. Even though Susanna was a victim in this painting, and her eyes betrayed the greatest fear, she was not defenseless. Gentileschi had painted a self-assurance on her face and body in the face of danger, a quality that would reappear in her later work and in many variations.

## Seductress and murderer in one

The curators of the Paris exhibition are particularly careful not to portray the artist as a victim who sought to process the humiliation of the assault in her work. This simplistic interpretation is tempting, given that Artemisia Gentileschi later frequently and masterfully varied the Old Testament story of Holofernes' murder by Judith . However, one should be wary of interpreting this as a form of revenge sublimated in art against her former rapist.

Here, too, one must look into Judith's eyes and read her body language in the different treatments of the subject. Resolute and serious at the same time, she beheads Holofernes with the sword; defiantly triumphant, she carries the sword on her shoulder after the deed is done; and Gentileschi regularly bathes Judith's face in harsh light, sharply dividing it into a light and a dark half. She is not an avenger, but the savior of the Jewish people, and she is both seductress and murderer. Only one thing is she not: a victim.

The theme of self-empowerment reaches its climax in Gentileschi's work with Cleopatra 's suicide. She painted several versions of this story, recounted by Plutarch, at different times. Perhaps the most striking is a painting created in the first half of the 1620s.

Gentileschi paints the Egyptian ruler naked, using his own model, and depicts her at the moment of death, as she brings an asp viper to her breast to receive its fatal bite. Cleopatra's face is divided. While her dying eyes gaze heavenward, her gaze already half-faded but still fully sane and alert, her mouth is slightly open. Her lips emerge from her softly shadowed face, provocatively full and luminous red. The hour of death is transformed here into an act of supreme sensuality.

Artemisia Gentileschi often bestows her own features upon her heroines—unless, as with the «Lute Player» of 1614/15, she is painting an explicit self-portrait. However, these are not egocentric self-dramatizations when she adorns Susanna, Judith, Cleopatra, or even a reclining Venus with her own attributes. Rather, she places the biblical narratives and even the ancient goddess within the backdrop of her own life.

Nothing human is alien to art, she seems to be saying. Above all, she also suggests that wherever ultimate questions are addressed—be it tyrannicide, love, or death—Gentileschi brings human beings to the canvas, not stereotypes. Every painting speaks directly to the viewer: You are the one being addressed; your story is being told here.

This is especially true for those images that depict humanity at its most human: as sinners. Artemisia Gentileschi painted a «Repentant Magdalene» several times. In Gentileschi's work, the iconic sinner of the New Testament becomes a woman who, while melancholic, is nevertheless self-aware and confident in her physical allure.

Nothing about her suggests any particular remorse, neither guilt nor repentance is evident; she bares her breast carelessly, not provocatively, and the fullness of her garments paradoxically suggests lust rather than chastity. This Magdalene may be aware of her sins, but she feels no guilt. For that seems to be what she wants to tell the observers: I am a human being, a sinner indeed, but a human being nonetheless.

## Caravaggio was the true teacher

One must imagine Artemisia Gentileschi as a self-assured woman. She knew that she painted better than most painters of her generation. And as bold as her pictorial inventions were, so too was her life. She traveled extensively, in Italy from Naples through Rome and Florence to Venice; in 1638 she visited her father in London and stayed there for two years.

She became pregnant five times, but only two children survived. Early on, she had a lover besides her husband, with his consent. To this emotional intelligence was added literary knowledge: she only learned to read and write as an adult, read Petrarch and Ovid, and wrote her own poetry.

And yet, at the end of her life, her trail goes cold: she likely died of the plague in Naples in 1656. Only gradually, in recent decades, has it become possible to reconstruct her life's work; many paintings are lost, others have been rediscovered. Some of her paintings are being exhibited in Paris for the first time.

Artemisia Gentileschi had learned her craft from her father. But even in her earliest works, she was far ahead of him. Recognizing this, alongside his determined support of his daughter, was perhaps Orazio Gentileschi's greatest achievement.

Meanwhile, Orazio belonged to a different generation, and his daughter had a new art before her. She found it in the paintings of Caravaggio, the revolutionary of the Baroque era; he, with the works accessible to Artemisia in Rome, was her true teacher. Before his paintings, she learned to look into the soul of people and fearlessly paint what she found there: human imperfection, which she united with the perfection of her painting.
