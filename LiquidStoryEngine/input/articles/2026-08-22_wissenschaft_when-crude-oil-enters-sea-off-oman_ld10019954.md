# When crude oil enters the sea off Oman, it is always an ecological catastrophe – what research has learned from past disasters

*Seabirds stuck together in oil, fish belly up – the immediate consequences of an oil spill are obvious. In the long term, however, life proves to be surprisingly resilient.*

For weeks, a largely unnoticed ecological drama has been unfolding in the Arabian Sea off the coast of Oman. The damaged supertanker «Caroline Bezengi» lies aground off the island of al-Kibliya. Huge quantities of crude oil are flowing into the sea from the ship, which is considered part of Russia's so-called shadow fleet. Experts estimate the cargo at more than 100,000 tons. It is difficult to say how much of it has already leaked. The resulting oil slick now covers an area of many hundreds of square kilometers, as can be clearly seen in satellite images. In mid-August, it reached the Omani coast.

The oil spill is unfolding in the midst of the Al Hallaniyyat Islands Nature Reserve, a strictly protected marine sanctuary. The islands are important breeding grounds for rare sea turtles and seabirds, and the waters boast coral reefs famous among dive tourists and a unique subspecies of humpback whale.

It is still too early to assess the potential damage to the ecosystem. What is clear is that crude oil is pure poison for all marine habitats, from the deep sea to the beach. «How severe the ecological consequences are in individual cases depends heavily on the abiotic conditions at the respective accident site,» says oceanographer Marcus Dengler from the GEOMAR Helmholtz Centre for Ocean Research Kiel. There, the physicist primarily focuses on ocean currents.

## Release point: crucial factors

First, the point of release. Crude oil that escapes at the seafloor, as in the Deepwater Horizon drilling platform blowout, can disperse significantly even in the water column. In shallow-water accidents near the coast, most of it quickly floats as an oil slick on the surface. There, the surf often whips it into a suspension of oil and water, similar to what you get when shaking a vinaigrette. Researchers affectionately call the result «Chocolate Mousse»: a viscous mass floating on the sea surface with many times the volume of the oil it contains. It is transported particularly far by ocean currents and makes it difficult to contain the oil's spread.

Whether moussed or not: «The further fate of the oil on the surface depends on large ocean currents and local wind conditions,» says Dengler. These can drive the oil out to the open sea, as was the case with the tanker «Castillo de Bellver» accident in 1983 off the coast of South Africa, which, despite more than 100,000 tons of oil released, had comparatively mild ecological consequences. This is because, when dispersed in the vast ocean, the hydrocarbon mixture is relatively quickly broken down by the influence of light and finally degraded by bacteria.

## Crude oil: a complex mixture

Wherever the journey takes it, light components of the crude oil evaporate along the way, while the remaining long-chain components clump together. «If these clumps become denser than seawater, they sink into the depths, and the oil slick continues to lose mass,» Dengler explains.

However, it is not a case of «out of sight, out of mind,» because in the depths, microbial degradation heavily consumes the often already scarce oxygen, thereby burdening the less obvious ecosystems, Dengler maintains. «In the current case in the Arabian Sea, this will be particularly interesting, as there are naturally large oxygen-free zones in the depths due to the lack of connection to cold, oxygen-rich ocean areas,» he adds.

Another effect also contributes to the oxygen deficiency, severely impairing the teeming life below the oil slick even on the open sea: it blocks the penetration of sunlight. The microalgae of the phytoplankton thus lack their basis for life, and additionally, because they live near the surface, they are also poisoned by soluble oil components. The consequence: the base of the entire food web collapses over large areas.

## Oil disasters: no fixed rules

Despite all degradation processes, the slick usually still contains more than enough oil when it finally reaches a coast. How severely it affects the local communities and how long it takes for them to overcome this damage depends on many factors: exposed rocky coasts suffer less than species-rich estuaries, and in polar seas, the microbial degradation of oil takes significantly longer due to low temperatures than in the tropics. «Every accident is different,» summarizes Marcus Dengler.

An exemplary look at five notorious oil disasters of the past illustrates some of the lessons that science has been able to draw so far:

## «Deepwater Horizon»: Gulf of Mexico (2010)

![The oil slick on the beach of Grand Isle, Louisiana, on May 22, 2010. The catastrophe also threatened the existence of many fishermen.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/18/b9850c09-a0c8-4eb1-9e78-fa8bd41856fa.jpg?crop=3000,1897,x0,y0)
*The oil slick on the beach of Grand Isle, Louisiana, on May 22, 2010. The catastrophe also threatened the existence of many fishermen.* (John Moore / Getty)

On April 20, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon drilling platform exploded in the Gulf of Mexico off New Orleans. By the time the well was temporarily sealed in July, at least half a million tons of crude oil had leaked out. «This accident differed in some important characteristics from typical tanker accidents,» says oceanographer Marcus Dengler. For example, the oil escaped at great depths, along with huge amounts of methane gas. It dispersed over large areas at a water depth of more than 1,000 meters, and a significant portion sank to the bottom.

But what rose to the surface was enough to contaminate more than 2,000 kilometers of the American coastline, including the Mississippi Delta and sensitive salt marshes. An overview study concluded six years after the accident that many communities had, however, recovered surprisingly well.

However, the consequences are not yet over for longer-lived animals at the top of the food pyramid, including large fish, dolphins, and sea turtles. A follow-up study published in 2020 found the tilefish to be particularly contaminated with harmful hydrocarbons: the fish dig extensive burrows into the sediment and thus regularly encounter submerged oil, the residues of which damage their internal organs.

## «Amoco Cadiz»: Brittany (1978)

![The bow of the «Amoco Cadiz» shortly after the accident in 1978. Today, all parts of the ship lie underwater and are a popular destination for divers.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/20/5c9c3e66-0f6c-455e-b389-1cc2b2e4c4d3.jpg?crop=3893,2576,x0,y0)
*The bow of the «Amoco Cadiz» shortly after the accident in 1978. Today, all parts of the ship lie underwater and are a popular destination for divers.* (Imago)

Today, the wreck of the supertanker «Amoco Cadiz» lies at a depth of 30 meters off the coast of Brittany. It ran aground on rocks on March 16, 1978, after a rudder failure, broke apart, and with its cargo of a good 220,000 tons of crude oil, caused one of the most serious oil disasters, directly off a European coast. The oil killed seabirds and marine animals along 360 kilometers of coastline, including thousands of tons of oysters. The recovery of the affected area followed the typical pattern of ecological succession: initially, a few species largely insensitive to the toxic hydrocarbons colonized the newly available space on rocks and sediments. Over the years, more and more species were added until, by the mid-1980s, biodiversity largely corresponded to what it had been before the accident.

## «Exxon Valdez»: Alaska (1989)

![After the «Exxon Valdez» accident, helpers cleaned the beach with hot steam – a serious mistake, as it turned out.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/18/74007f4b-d0e0-4b8d-8baa-32be6a72a050.jpg?crop=5315,3543,x0,y0)
*After the «Exxon Valdez» accident, helpers cleaned the beach with hot steam – a serious mistake, as it turned out.* (Jean-Louis Atlan / Sygma)

Hardly any oil spill has been the subject of as many ecological impact studies as the accident of the tanker «Exxon Valdez,» which ran aground on a reef off the coast of South Alaska on March 24, 1989. Approximately 40,000 tons of leaked crude oil covered about 2,000 kilometers of pristine coastline with a brown slick.

Seabirds, but also sea otters and other marine mammals, died by the thousands. Some holes in the ecological network closed quickly on their own, while others remained open for decades. For example, the bladderwrack, which had previously served as habitat and food for countless animals, was only able to re-establish itself very slowly over large areas: its place on the rocks had in the meantime been occupied by other algae and barnacles.

In many places, the bladderwrack was less a victim of the oil than of the cleaning measures. After the accident, attempts were made to remove the oil from the coast using steam cleaners. This was successful, but it also successfully removed all life from the rocks and pebble beaches. Since then, cleaning operations have been carried out with considerably more restraint.

## Oil Spill in the Second Gulf War: Kuwait (1991)

![Beach in Kuwait shortly after Iraqi forces caused the largest oil spill of all time. Burning oil wells blaze in the background.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/08/18/4296bc64-c0bf-490e-b0b3-02cb283d4c32.jpg?crop=4748,3300,x0,y0)
*Beach in Kuwait shortly after Iraqi forces caused the largest oil spill of all time. Burning oil wells blaze in the background.* (Allan Tannenbaum / Archive Photos / Getty)

To hinder the landing of coalition forces, the Iraqi army channeled an estimated over one million tons of oil into the Persian Gulf during the Second Gulf War – the largest oil spill of all time. Salt marshes and mangrove belts died off over large areas – and with them their typical animal communities. At first glance, many of the affected ecosystems quickly recovered from the acute damage. But even here, the oil remains deep in the sediment to this day. Moreover, the Persian Gulf never truly recovers: large quantities of crude oil also entered the tropical sea during recent hostilities.

## Oil Spill in Bahia las Minas: Panama 1986

A scientific peculiarity is the oil spill of just 8,000 tons of oil that flowed from a ruptured storage tank into Bahia las Minas on the Caribbean side of Panama on April 27, 1986. Affected areas included coral reefs, sensitive seagrass beds, and mangrove forests, which died off over approximately 70 hectares. What is special is that the Galeta Marine Laboratory of the Smithsonian Institution is located in the middle of the affected region, from which biologists have been studying life in and around the surrounding sea since the 1960s.

For this reason, researchers were immediately on site and could draw on extensive datasets about the state of the ecosystem before the accident – a unique scientific opportunity that led to numerous publications. For example, a study recently published in the «Bulletin of Marine Science» shows that the mangrove stands regenerating in the areas affected in 1986 thrived less well for up to 20 years than those in unaffected areas.
