# No more boozing to boost a career? Why alcohol consumption in China is tumbling

*Business in China has long been associated with marathon drinking sessions. Younger people now want different options. Even the government has declared war on traditional Chinese spirits – but not out of concern for public health.*

**Katrin Büchenbacher** · NZZ in English · 2025-06-06 · 2379 words · [ld.1887552](https://www.nzz.ch/english/chinas-once-famous-drinking-culture-is-drying-out-ld.1887552)

> Summary
> -  Alcohol consumption patterns in China are shifting, with younger people drinking less and shifting to lighter beverages like beer instead of spirits.
> - The marathon drinking sessions once frequently found in business circles are becoming less common, with career success now depending less on drinking rituals.
> - Political measures such as anti-corruption campaigns are also contributing to the decline in alcohol consumption.

China has an alcohol problem. Or actually two. And both are associated with men: Older men are drinking too much liquor, and younger men are drinking too little.

According to a long-term study published in 2019 by The Lancet, a scientific journal , men are the main consumers of alcohol in China. The study found that 33% of men drank alcohol every week, mostly spirits, compared to only 2% of women.

Out of ambition and to demonstrate their masculinity, men sometimes drink until they vomit – to impress business partners, to entertain their bosses, or to demonstrate their integrity and loyalty. For a long time, it was said in China that if you wanted to get ahead in your career, you had to be able to hold your drink. This expectation has sometimes even been openly stated in job postings. Men wanting to make a mark in the business world have also been expected to smoke. Nearly 50% of the world's cigarettes are sold in China. However, these practices are now changing.

## Drinking for different reasons

Young men in China today are drinking and smoking less. In a survey recently cited in an article in the Shanghai newspaper Pengpai, 80% of young respondents said they preferred to drink only small amounts of alcohol. Sixty percent said they spent less than 1,000 yuan per year on alcohol – the equivalent of just 115 Swiss francs, or about $140. In China, that would buy around 140 bottles of beer, or three to four bottles of regular baijiu, the traditional Chinese grain spirit.

It seems that young people are thus showing a preference for comparatively low-alcohol beer over the much stronger baijiu. This is reflected in production volumes. The major baijiu producers in China together produced almost 13.6 billion tons of the spirit in 2016. By 2023, volumes had fallen to only a third of that. During the same period, per capita consumption of the beverage fell by more than 50%.

![Baijiu, the Chinese grain spirit, is regarded as a cultural treasure. A single bottle can cost the equivalent of several hundred dollars. But inexpensive homemade spirits are also available.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/05/30/d03ed899-c38c-4bc3-afe0-3e535fd1dd72.jpeg?width=1024)

What happened to make the Chinese lose their taste for spirits? The trend is being driven by two key factors – although health concerns are not as yet playing a major role.

Hu Yang has seen this happen firsthand. He is in the spirits business. For a long time, the 31-year-old from Shanxi province sold baijiu in his parents' shop. With an alcohol content of up to 60% by volume, the liquor tends to burn sharply in the throat on a drinker's first sip. Hu's typical customers were middle-aged men who bought baijiu for their sons' weddings as gifts, or for their own consumption. Today, Hu no longer sells spirits. Instead, he works in Beijing as an advertising photographer for China's major liquor manufacturers.

«Young people prefer to drink casually with friends in a bar rather than with their boss, work colleagues or business partners,» says Hu in a video interview. Beer, cocktails and wine are the popular drinks when people are looking to relax in their free time, he notes. The ritualized, heavy drinking of high-proof spirits in a professional context has become less common, he adds. Today, competence counts more than a person's ability to hold their drink, Hu says.

Young Chinese people today want to make their own decisions. They want to be free to choose whether, how much and with whom they drink. Many no longer want to bow to the older generation's strict social rules and expectations.

The country's alcohol industry is consequently worried about further declines, and is employing unconventional methods to get young people interested in drinking. For example, the baijiu brand Moutai came up with the idea of ​​adding a shot of baijiu to coffee or to bubble tea. These are both trendy drinks among young people. Large coffeehouse and bubble tea chains in China have joined in. As a result, Moutai, the world's most valuable alcohol brand, has experienced a resurgence.

## Keeping the glass full

That's correct: The most valuable alcohol brand in the world is not Budweiser, Jack Daniel's or Hennessy. It is Moutai, and it comes from China. Moutai is a spirit in a class of its own. The cheapest bottle costs the equivalent of nearly $75, with prices more typically ranging between $450 and nearly $1,000. A bottle of Moutai that has been aged for 50 years might cost around $6,000.

Chinese people almost never buy Moutai for themselves. Rather, it tends to be a gift: for a future father-in-law, a boss or business partner, or occasionally for civil servants, police officers or Communist Party officials from whom the gift-giver hopes to receive a favor. The bottle is then shared during a meal together. Each glass is always consumed at one go, and a small toast and clinking of glasses are mandatory. It isn't necessary to look into each other's eyes, as is often done in German-speaking countries. Nevertheless, drinking in China follows clear rules.

When toasting one another, for example, someone who is comparatively lower down in the table's hierarchy can indicate their social status by placing the rim of their glass approximately in the middle of the glass of the person opposite them. If the guest of honor wants to drink, it is important to make sure that his glass is never empty. This is how he is honored.

Drinking spirits helps to build relationships. Maybe the drinkers even get so drunk that they hug each other, laugh together, slur their words or stumble down the stairs. They have experienced something intense together. In the best case, such activities help to generate trust, closeness and friendship. Friends help one another, and communicate more directly than is otherwise possible. That makes everything easier. And if someone does embarrass themselves, they can always blame it on the alcohol the next day.

## No longer a guarantee of success

The consumption of spirits is seen as most important in the Chinese business world. Work-related feasts usually take place after the working day is done, when everyone goes out to a restaurant together. The drinking there sometimes turns into a competition, typically among men. Those who can hold more drink gain respect – and thus improve their negotiating positions within the company.

Zhu Longwei, 29, runs a factory outside Shanghai that produces hygiene products such as incontinence pads. When he started his own business a few years ago, after graduating from university, he was constantly on the go. «During the startup phase, I was constantly meeting with the industry's ‹old boys.› Back then, there was a lot of pressure to eat and drink with customers,» Zhu says. «I've gotten used to the drinking binges. It's become part of the routine.»

Zhu says he personally likes to drink a mixture of two-thirds beer and one-third rice wine. That's a common cocktail near Shanghai, where he lives. The stronger baijiu is also served on business occasions. However, Zhu says he rarely gets drunk. He doesn't like feeling completely drained of energy the next day. Anyway, people no longer expect him to be drunk, he says. «In the 1990s, it was still ‹If you drink the glass, the deal is sealed,›» Zhu says. «Today, it's much more about the quality and price of the product that you want to sell.»

However, this doesn't mean it's easy to get by without drinking at all. Zhu admits that alcohol still serves as a kind of catalyst, and that social drinking still helps careers. «If I want to buy a product, and both Company A and Company B offer a similar product at a similar price and with similar quality, I'll naturally buy from the company that I have a better personal relationship with,» the businessman says. In the end, China is still a relationship-based society, Zhu notes.

## A ban on alcohol

Alcohol also has the advantage of loosening the tongue. It leads tough-skinned businessmen to talk about their private lives and reveal their hidden desires. In doing so, alcohol also opens the door to bribery.

When Chinese President Xi Jinping came to power in 2012, he cracked down on booze-soaked celebrations as part of his anti-corruption campaign. He banned military, government and civil service employees from drinking alcohol during working hours or at official banquets. Employees of state-owned companies also had to comply with this rule. Xi also banned the purchase of expensive Moutai spirits with public funds.

This is another reason why the Chinese drink less high-proof liquor today. They are afraid of being considered corrupt. However, consumption also seems to have shifted somewhat: Civil servants, officials and managers of state-owned companies are simply drinking more French red wine. At home, much the same rules apply as for drinking spirits. It has to be an expensive bottle from Bordeaux, and the wineglass must be emptied completely after each toast. This way, people can still get drunk quickly even without drinking any high proof liquors.

The government is now trying to close the last remaining loopholes. Even foreign diplomats are no longer allowed to ply Chinese officials with alcohol. According to the Beijing News platform, a new directive issued in late May states that extravagant foods, cigarettes and alcohol are not allowed to be served at official receptions or working meals. With rules such as this, Xi wants to put a stop to the «waste» of public funds.

It wasn't always this way. Legend has it that former President Jiang Zemin once downed 25 shots of high-proof Moutai at a single session. When the founder of the People's Republic of China, Mao Zedong, met with US President Richard Nixon and his adviser Henry Kissinger in 1972, Moutai was also famously served. Kissinger is said to have remarked that all problems could be solved with Moutai. It may be that he simply wanted to flutter his Chinese hosts, however. For it is also said that he warned Nixon about this «deadly brew,» which, he supposedly said, was not used as jet fuel only because it was too flammable.

## «We’re trying to get pregnant»

Health concerns seem to be playing a relatively minor role in the public's decision to abstain from alcohol. However, increased alcohol consumption since the 2000s has had a public health impact within China. A study published in the scientific journal Nature concluded that men who drink alcohol in China are facing an elevated risk for more than 60 diseases, including mental illness, cancer and liver disease. Associated societal effects include car accidents, an increase in domestic violence and rising divorce rates. The high smoking rate is also proving harmful to the Chinese. About 35% of deaths among men in the country are linked to tobacco use.

Nevertheless, the Chinese state is more liberal than other countries when it comes to alcohol. The consumption tax on alcohol is relatively low, and does not distinguish between beer and spirits. Advertising for baijiu is everywhere in China: on television, in online series and on screens in elevators. Minors can get their hands on alcohol without any problems. When it comes to drivers, however, the state has a zero-tolerance policy: Anyone who drinks even one glass of an alcoholic beverage and drives will lose their driver's license in China.

Thus, «I have to drive» is a good reason to abstain from drinking alcohol in China. «We're trying to get pregnant» is another. In China, this applies to men as well as women. To increase the chances of conceiving, it is said there that men too should avoid alcohol. The latest trend in the country is alcohol-free wines. According to Chinese customs statistics, imports of nonalcoholic beverages rose by more than 25% in the first quarter of 2025. Young Chinese people cannot do completely without the drinking sessions with the «old boys,» because these remain useful in furthering their careers. But they want to keep their wits about them.

The soul of Chinese alcohol remains baijiu, however. This is not available in a nonalcoholic version. «Everything else I wanted to say is in the wine,» the Chinese like to say when they want to express their deepest feelings. They then clink and drain their glasses.
