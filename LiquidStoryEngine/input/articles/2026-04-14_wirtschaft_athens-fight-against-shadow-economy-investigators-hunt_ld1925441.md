# Athens' fight against the shadow economy: Investigators hunt for tax evaders

*Greek tax morale long had a bad reputation. With the digitalization of the tax system, the government is now taking rigorous action against fraudsters – with considerable progress.*

Panagiotis Kintzios knows that no one is happy to see him cross their threshold. Being a tax inspector in a country with a low tax morale isn't always fun. «We're not exactly welcome,» he says drily, with a smile. Nevertheless, the random check he's conducting with his colleague Panagiota Stasi doesn't make him nervous. This morning, their destination is a traditional coffeehouse that seems to have been frozen in time: heavy curtains, upholstered chairs, plastic tables, yellowed pictures, large TV screens – a mismatched collection. On the terrace sit old regulars – bespectacled men with thinning hair, smoking and drinking coffee, marveling at the visit from the tax authorities. Kintzios and Stasi introduce themselves to the waitress, showing their official identification and the inspection order. This is important, Stasi explains: «There are fraudsters who impersonate tax inspectors.»

A guest immediately interjects. «Why do you come here? Why don't you check the big fish?» Kintzios politely points out the duty of his job. «We're also psychologists in this role,» he had said earlier. He added that guests complain about their presence more often and provoke unpleasant situations more than the staff – especially when alcohol is involved.

Kintzios and Stasi access the coffee shop's tax data on a tablet; in just a few clicks, they have an overview. They sift through the transactions, checking the receipts issued at each individual table. Because cash register systems in Greece are now required to be networked with the tax authorities, any misuse is quickly detected. This digitalization has led to less tax evasion, Stasi explains.

## Replica fuel pump for training purposes

That was also the government's goal. Tax evasion and the shadow economy were considered one of the causes of the Greek financial crisis. At its peak in 2013, the informal economy was estimated to account for between 23 and 30 percent of GDP, depending on the study and methodology. «The crisis forced us to reform the tax system,» explains Giorgos Pitsilis, head of the tax authority AADE. He has been in the business for years and has overhauled the tax administration, notorious for its inefficiency. He has led the agency since it was separated from the Ministry of Finance in 2017 and began operating independently – a demand of the international creditors.

Pitsilis has digitized the tax system. By introducing a digital accounting system, transactions are monitored in real time by the tax authorities, and bank data is linked to the administration. In addition to developing digital tools, controls have been tightened, a cash limit has been introduced, and electronic payments have been promoted.

The progress is impressive. According to the government, the measures generated an additional €2.2 billion in revenue for the state last year, contributing to the country's economic recovery, with total net government revenues reaching €77 billion. While the size of the shadow economy, at 21.9 percent of GDP, remains high by European standards, it is declining. This is also reflected in the shrinking VAT gap, the difference between the theoretically owed and the actual amount of VAT paid: According to the European Commission, it has decreased by two-thirds since 2017 and stood at 10.5 percent in 2023. The EU average is 7 percent.

Pitsilis explains how digitalization enables more targeted inspections across the country. In small teams, a total of 2,000 inspectors like Kintzios and Stasi conduct random checks of restaurants, law offices, supermarkets, and gas stations, primarily when there is concrete evidence of abuse. They receive such evidence from the «boarding room» of the modern AADE operations center in Athens.

In the sparsely furnished control room – everything is bright and white – eight large screens hang on the wall. They display: real-time images from a drone inspecting a tanker truck on a highway, footage of a private yacht, a map showing the current locations of the investigation teams, and satellite images of «suspicious targets.» Half a dozen data analysts sit at rows of tables behind computers in front of them.

One of them is evaluating the latest risk profiles generated by the artificial intelligence. The focus is on several gas stations whose data on delivered, stored, and sold fuel quantities shows discrepancies. AADE monitors these automatically and in real time. Using a color-coded cluster diagram, the analyst examines the network of gas stations: owners, accountants, lawyers, and other companies involved. «If the indications of misuse become more concrete, we dispatch several investigation teams with the support of other authorities, coordinating and monitoring their operations,» explains Konstantia Tachtsidou, head of the control room.

The current suspected case is said to be typical: The gasoline trade reportedly has the largest shadow economy in Greece. In light of this, the AADE has built a replica of a gas pump specifically for training purposes. They practice using it to detect technical tampering. A tanker truck is currently being replicated for the same purpose. The responsible official proudly displays the plans.

## Houses under constant construction and dwindling living space

Pitsilis, the head of the tax authority, has no illusions. Digitalization, which offers the state new possibilities in the fight against tax evasion, also benefits fraudsters. «We're in a constant race,» he says. He notes that the state has now seen through many old tricks, but new ones are sure to follow, and they have to be prepared for that.

The «old tricks» are still talked about in Greece. Particularly in the restaurant and service sectors, cash payments were long the norm, and receipts were avoided to evade VAT and keep declared income low. Until a few years ago, 70 percent of self-employed individuals also behaved conspicuously, declaring income below the legal minimum wage. Creativity was also evident in the real estate sector: houses officially remained «under construction,» facades unplastered, roofs unfinished, all to pay less tax. For the same reason, living space was reduced by a few square meters on paper, and some swimming pools existed in the garden but were not registered in the land registry.

Electronic cash register systems, house mapping, a minimum tax for the self-employed, and digital reporting systems limit the scope for such maneuvers. Nevertheless, their ingenuity is astonishing. Where does this low tax morale come from? «Perhaps it's in our DNA,» says Tachtsidou, the head of the tax office, with self-deprecating humor. Her agency's task, she explains, is to make the public understand that paying taxes is an obligation. «Many think it's optional.»

Tachtsidou used to conduct inspections herself, experiencing bribery attempts and unpleasant incidents. Even today, she notices how people avoid her in private as soon as they learn about her profession. «They become suspicious,» she says with a laugh. She adds that while the tax morale of Greeks has improved, there is still room for improvement. In her opinion, the root cause is the Greeks' distrust of their government. According to a Eurobarometer survey, 79% of the population distrusts the state. Pitsilis has an explanation for this, which he phrases diplomatically: «It's related to how people perceive the state's handling of their money.»

Yannis Marinos is a good example. The 46-year-old sits on the terrace of his restaurant in Athens' Plaka district, enjoying a coffee and a cigarette, and says: «You get the feeling you're not getting anything back.» It's a statement that resonates. Marinos makes it in connection with a recent hospital visit. He accompanied a friend there. The dilapidated infrastructure shocked the friendly man so much that he says he would rather die than have to be treated there.

Marinos implemented the electronic cash register system in his restaurant in 2024, as required by law. He supports the government's approach. He admits that the problem of undeclared income hasn't disappeared, but it has decreased. Nevertheless, he's annoyed. Unprompted, he brings up the massive embezzlement of EU subsidies: For years, the state has been evading billions, he fumes. «Then these same people come to me, checking the receipt for every single coffee, as if I were a thief.» It's not about the checks themselves, but about the principle.

He believes the 24 percent value-added tax is excessive. How the government calculates it is a mystery to him. He calls it «Greek mathematics.» This makes survival difficult, he says. He has to absorb costs somewhere, but at the same time, he can't afford to drive away customers. In his view, an even stricter control system with a lower tax rate would be fairer.

## Lottery and amateur tax investigators

Marinos is not the only one complaining about the high VAT. Small businesses, especially mechanics and hair salons, are struggling with it. It is whispered that tradespeople are still reluctant to issue receipts. Pitsilis knows this too. «We can't just barge into a house and check if the plumber has issued an invoice.» He adds jokingly, «Unfortunately, we're not invited to doctor's appointments either. People go alone.» He therefore appeals to the common good, adding that the fight against tax evasion requires everyone's cooperation.

The government is also actively involving the population, for example with a «tax lottery.» This aims to encourage people to pay without cash. For every bill paid digitally, there's a ticket that automatically participates in a monthly drawing. 556 prizes are up for grabs each month.

He's also turning the population into amateur tax inspectors. To this end, he launched the app «appodixi,» whose name combines «app» and the Greek word for receipt. Using this app, any citizen can scan the receipt they receive and check whether the full amount has been reported to the tax office. In 2024, 75,000 reports were received. More than half of these indicated tax evasion.

Kintzios and Stasi spend a little more time in the coffee shop than planned. The digital cash register system refuses to issue a receipt due to connection problems with the server. The waitress tries to reason with the machine: «Come on, don't do this to me, I don't want to be led away in handcuffs.» Slightly flustered, she compliments Stasi's smile as she waits patiently. The most important thing, Stasi says, is to always treat people kindly and respectfully – even if they cheat. In the case of the coffee shop, that's not the case. The cash register system eventually issues the receipt, and there's nothing to complain about.
