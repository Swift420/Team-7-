# The Indians triumphed at the Little Bighorn River – but their downfall was nonetheless inevitable

*150 years ago, US troops suffered a crushing defeat against the Sioux and Cheyenne. Under the leadership of Sitting Bull, these tribes fought to survive their nomadic way of life. Their descendants have still not overcome the trauma of subjugation.*

On June 27, 1876, General Philip Sheridan in Chicago received a disturbing report from his subordinate, General Alfred Terry, in Montana. Sheridan was the commander-in-chief in the war against the last free Plains Indians. Terry wrote: «It is my painful duty to report that on the 25th of this month, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer and the troops under his command were struck by a great disaster.»

Terry further wrote that Custer's 7th U.S. Cavalry Regiment had attacked a large Sioux and Cheyenne encampment on the Little Bighorn River from two sides. Three companies launched a surprise attack on the village from the south across the river in the early afternoon. Five more companies, under Custer's command, were to bypass the encampment and then launch the main attack from the north. While attempting to cross the river, Custer was repulsed by the Native Americans and forced to retreat to a nearby hill. His troops were then surrounded. Custer and 209 men were killed by the Native Americans.

The attackers in the south were also driven back across the river by a counterattack and forced to entrench themselves. The Native Americans besieged them until noon the following day. Afterward, they broke camp and allowed the survivors to withdraw. General Terry reached the battlefield shortly afterward. A total of 263 men from the 7th Cavalry Regiment were killed and at least 50 seriously wounded in the fighting. On the Native American side, 40 to 60 were killed and an unknown number were wounded.

## Fight against white land seizure

General Sheridan's adversary was the Hunkpapa Sioux leader Sitting Bull, who was born around 1831 near the Missouri River in South Dakota. The western Sioux tribes – also known as Lakota after their language – and the Cheyenne lived as nomads on the northern American prairie at that time. They were warrior peoples who constantly fought with their neighbors for the best hunting grounds. At the heart of their lives was the bison, of which there were once 40 to 60 million in North America. It not only provided them with their primary food source in the form of meat, but they also utilized virtually every part of the animal for their daily lives.

![Sitting Bull, chief of the Hunkpapa Sioux and leader of the Sioux and Cheyenne in their last war with the U.S. Army in 1876/77.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/05/12/5d3813d6-c16a-4e65-9a61-cd9f681df844.jpg?crop=2415,4084,x0,y0)
*Sitting Bull, chief of the Hunkpapa Sioux and leader of the Sioux and Cheyenne in their last war with the U.S. Army in 1876/77.* (Library of Congress / Corbis Historical / Getty)

Even in his early youth, Sitting Bull distinguished himself as an excellent warrior and in 1857 became war chief of the Hunkpapa Sioux. At this time, white settlers began migrating into Lakota territory, a development he resisted with raids. After a two-year war with the U.S. Army, a majority of the Lakota and their Cheyenne allies finally signed the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868. This treaty established the Great Sioux Reservation, which essentially encompassed present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River. They were granted exclusive rights to this territory.

![The signing of the Treaty of Laramie in 1868. It created the Great Sioux Reservation, where the Plains Indians were to become sedentary.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/05/12/950a1eb3-aa29-4780-8edb-0d84f92c1b9f.jpg?crop=3732,2680,x0,y0)
*The signing of the Treaty of Laramie in 1868. It created the Great Sioux Reservation, where the Plains Indians were to become sedentary.* (Bettmann / Getty)

In return, the Native Americans had to settle on the reservation under the agency responsible for their tribe and henceforth live on government food rations. The bison was deliberately exterminated to deprive the nomadic Native Americans of their livelihood and to clear the prairie for settlement by white settlers.

## Sitting Bull's War

In the summer of 1874, an expedition under Lieutenant Colonel Custer discovered gold in the Black Hills within the reservation. This led to an invasion of gold prospectors into the territory reserved for Native Americans and to renewed conflict with the Lakota and Cheyenne. In November 1875, U.S. President Ulysses Grant decided to force the remaining Traditionals onto the reservation. He issued them an ultimatum to report to the agencies responsible for them there by January 31, 1876.

The ultimatum was a thinly veiled declaration of war. The Native Americans were in their winter camps in Montana, and many children and elderly people would have been unlikely to survive a march to their agencies in South Dakota through the freezing weather and deep snow.

After the ultimatum expired unheeded, General Sheridan ordered a campaign against the Traditionals in their strongholds south of the Yellowstone River in early February. Three troop contingents advanced into the area from the south, east and west. They were reinforced by Native American scouts from tribes hostile to the Sioux. On March 17, the vanguard of the southern contingent, under the command of General George Crook, attacked a Cheyenne camp on the Powder River in the early morning. The attack came as a complete surprise to the approximately 500 inhabitants. Although they managed to escape, the soldiers burned the tipis and destroyed their food supplies and belongings.

Thus began the last great war against the Plains Indians. Sitting Bull sent messengers urging the nomadic groups of Traditionals, as well as the warriors on the reservations, to join him at a large summer camp on the Rosebud River. With the unification of several thousand Native Americans, he hoped to be able to resist the U.S. Army.

On June 17, the first major battle took place outside the Rosebud camp between General Crook's troops and the united Traditionals. Although outnumbered, the latter managed to halt Crook's forces. After the six-hour battle, the general returned to his base camp in Wyoming to tend to the wounded and replenish depleted supplies, where he waited for reinforcements for seven weeks.

The Traditionals moved on to the Little Bighorn River. The camp had by then grown to around 7,000 people, among them an estimated 1,800 warriors. Despite General Crook's withdrawal, the danger was not over, as General Terry's troops were approaching from the east. Thus, in the early afternoon of June 25, Custer's 7th Cavalry Regiment launched a surprise attack, which ended in a catastrophic defeat for the U.S. Army.

## Campaign of annihilation

But the victory over Custer did little good for the Native Americans. Sitting Bull knew that the U.S. Army would now seek revenge with new troops. He could not maintain the enormous camp much longer. Providing for so many people and ponies in one place for an extended period was impossible. Furthermore, the camp, with its many children, women and elderly people, was too immobile to defend itself against or evade attacks from new American troops. It therefore dispersed into numerous groups. Many warriors from the reservations returned to their families.

At his headquarters in Chicago, General Sheridan now planned a total war against the last nomadic Lakota and Cheyenne. He had a series of new forts built in Montana, from which he launched relentless attacks against the camps of the remaining Traditionals. He imposed military control on the Native Americans on the reservations, forcing them to surrender the weapons and ponies they had retained to exercise their hunting rights under the 1868 treaty.

*Photo taken in Sitting Bull's camp in 1875.* (Smith Collection / Gado / Archive Photos / Getty)

The situation became increasingly difficult for the Traditionals. White bison hunters were now decimating the remaining herds in Montana as well. The Native Americans lost their livelihoods and began to suffer from hunger. Furthermore, their camps were repeatedly destroyed by the U.S. Army. The inhabitants were forced to flee without supplies or belongings. Twice, in October and December 1876, this also happened to Sitting Bull's camp. The winter of 1876/77 became a struggle for survival. In the new year, the last Traditionals had only two options: surrender on the reservation or flee to Canada, where they would be safe from the U.S. Army.

## Exile in Canada

At the end of April 1877, Sitting Bull crossed the border into Canada with around 900 followers. The refugees were greeted kindly by the Royal Mounted Police. However, it was made clear to them that they were now subject to local laws and would not receive food rations from Canada. The latter was not necessary at the moment. In the border region with the U.S., where Sitting Bull's followers found a new home, a large herd of bison provided sufficient food for the time being.

Within months, however, the number of refugees from the U.S. swelled to several thousand. They became serious competition for the Canadian First Nations. The bison herds were overexploited. The following year, bison became rare even in southern Canada. Hunger returned. Fearing a conflict with the U.S., Canada now pressed for the refugees to return to its southern neighbor. In the following years, more and more Traditionals returned there and surrendered.

On July 19, 1881, Sitting Bull finally surrendered with a last group of nearly 200 people at Fort Buford on the Missouri River. It was a sad sight, a local journalist wrote: people in rags, some almost naked, with the harrowing expressions of those who had not eaten enough for months. Upon surrendering his weapon, Sitting Bull wished only to be remembered as the last of his tribe to give up his rifle.

It was a humiliating scene for the Native American leader. All his life he had been his own master. Now he and his people were taken to the Standing Rock Agency, which was responsible for them. He and his followers had to abandon their traditional way of life and submit to the white administrator and the army.

## Last years on the reservation

The bison was on the brink of extinction. Its numbers plummeted from 40 to 60 million around 1800 to just a few thousand by the mid-1880s. Paradoxically, it was none other than General Sheridan who contributed to the species' survival. One of the last small herds survived in Yellowstone National Park, established in 1872. It became the starting point for building a new population. In 1886, Sheridan sent the army into the park to keep out intruders. The army managed the park for 30 years, ensuring the species' survival. Today, there are once again half a million animals.

After his surrender, Sitting Bull and 172 followers were held as prisoners of war for 19 months. The Americans thus broke a promise they had previously made to him. Six smaller reservations were carved out of the Great Sioux Reservation, and the rest was opened up for settlement by white settlers. After his release, Sitting Bull lived with his followers on the Standing Rock Reservation of the Hunkpapa Sioux. He engaged in some farming and, as a legendary war hero, toured the United States with a Wild West show.

He never adapted to a settled life. He once told a missionary that he would rather die as a Native American than live as a white man. On December 15, 1890, he was shot and killed by Native American police officers during an attempt to arrest him. The white administrator of the reservation had feared that Sitting Bull might incite a rebellion as part of a Native American religious revival movement.

## Unresolved trauma

The descendants of Sitting Bull's Hunkpapa Sioux have still not overcome the trauma of losing their traditional way of life. Many continue to live on the Standing Rock Reservation. Nearly 80% are unemployed. Alcoholism and high suicide rates are rampant. The poverty rate is around 50%, almost four times the national average. The healthcare system is chronically underfunded, contributing to a low life expectancy.

Outside of reservation administration and local education and healthcare, there are few jobs. The Sioux lack sufficient fertile land and access to credit for successful agriculture. Those with better education can hope for advancement in American cities, but if they leave the reservation, they risk losing their connection to their people and their cultural roots.
