# Unique as an athlete and as a person – Mikaela Shiffrin breaks all boundaries with her 100th World Cup victory

*The American ski racer achieved a historic milestone in the Sestriere slalom, setting new standards. But Mikaela Shiffrin is far more than just an athlete. A chronicle of many encounters off the slopes.*

Mikaela Shiffrin has now amassed 100 victories in the Ski World Cup, having won the slalom in Sestriere on Sunday. The record of 86 wins held by Sweden's Ingemar Stenmark, which stood for 34 years, almost seems ordinary in comparison.

Shiffrin is the best female ski racer ever to hurtle down the icy slopes of the World Cup, but that's not what this text is about. I've had the privilege, over twelve years and in countless encounters, of witnessing how this American stands out from the rest as a person and personality, just as much as she does as an outstanding athlete. That's what makes her unique.

It's late November 2012 in Beaver Creek. Beat Feuz is in the hospital with a damaged knee, and the Swiss ski crisis is beginning. It's a welcome relief to be invited to a dinner by a pasta company. The hostess is Mikaela Shiffrin, who lives down in the valley, then 17 years old with only one podium finish to her name. She stands at the entrance to the ballroom in an evening gown, flanked by her brother Taylor in a tuxedo, and shakes hands with each guest, saying, «Nice to meet you.»

Before the main course, Shiffrin stands in the middle of the room and talks about herself, her experiences, her goals. I'm astonished. There stands a teenager, the tables are full of older men, journalists with hundreds of World Cup races under their belts, and Shiffrin speaks completely unfazed, without hesitation or faltering. She's in this role just as she was back then on the slopes: far ahead of her age.

Three weeks later, she won her first World Cup race; in February 2013, Shiffrin became World Champion, and a year later, Olympic Champion. She was the outstanding slalom skier, captivating audiences with her effortless grace. At 18, Shiffrin had already won nine World Cup races, two season slalom titles, and the two most important global championships. That's more than many others achieve in an entire career. She was called a prodigy, the Mozart of skiing.

## When Shiffrin executes a perfect run, she feels like she's dancing

That was a topic when we first sat down for an interview in October 2013. She chatted away nonstop, laughed out loud at some of the questions, and gave such thoughtful and nuanced answers as if I had sent her the list of questions days in advance so she could prepare.

Over the years, it became clear that Shiffrin was always like that. Even when, as at the 2022 Winter Games, she spoke into the microphones with tears in her eyes after a string of disappointments, it sounded as if she had analyzed every point the night before and clearly formulated her thoughts on it.

We talked in 2014 about Mozart, whom she knew from classical concerts she had attended with her father. Shiffrin described how she puts on headphones when she visualizes a slalom course; the music then sets the rhythm of her mental run. And when she manages a perfect run on the slope, it actually feels like dancing. But no: «I'm not a prodigy.» What she has achieved is the product of a lot of hard work and support.

A few years later, I was sitting with her mother on a bench in the sun in Austria, talking about our children and parenthood. Eileen Shiffrin told me how she fostered her children's natural love of sports. Her son Taylor wanted to play soccer but didn't make the school team, so she bought small goals and spent hours playing with her children during the summer holidays.

Taylor also raced skis, and his little sister followed in his footsteps. She did it with such dedication that she would even ski lap after lap in the rain, completely focused. Their mother had the idea of ​​buying her a unicycle to improve her balance, and later she gave her juggling balls. And so Mikaela Shiffrin cruised around the neighborhood on her unicycle, juggling balls. «The neighbors must have thought we were completely crazy,» said Eileen.

The conversation revolves around the question of why outstanding athletes often work closely with their parents, such as Marcel Hirscher, Lara Gut-Behrami, and Mikaela Shiffrin, who always shared a room with her mother when traveling for World Cup events. It's about a deep trust, Eileen said. «Do you think Lara needs her father? Pauli is there for her because the two of them have a great relationship. The same is true for Marcel Hirscher and his father.»

After her first World Championship victory, the then 17-year-old Mikaela Shiffrin said at the press conference: «I need my mom.» The American Ski Federation refused to acknowledge this for a long time, and the family had to pay all travel and hotel expenses out of their own pockets. This reportedly cost half a million dollars in total.

Later, the mother was accepted as part of the small Shiffrin team within the federation. With her keen eye, she immediately recognized her daughter's slightest weaknesses. And Eileen sometimes played the role of the merciless critic. Many coaches didn't dare to address mistakes bluntly, she says. «I can do that because our relationship can withstand it.»

For years, Eileen Shiffrin was part of a success machine that seemed unstoppable in the skiing world. As Mikaela had already said in that 2014 interview, the engine was hard work, hard work, hard work. Her father, Jeff, once said that his daughter never skied simply for fun; every single turn involved a task – even the few meters from the lift to the starting gate.

## «This is Emma, ​​she has leukemia.»

But the athlete never isolated herself in this professional world, and she understood early on that while sport is important, there are other things in life. More important things. We talked about this in Méribel in 2015; Shiffrin had just won the slalom discipline title for the third time in a row. A week earlier in Åre, she had met Emma Lundell again, whom she had met in 2012 during her first victory. Lundell was a little girl back then who approached Shiffrin at the end of an autograph session. The child's mother said, «This is Emma; she has leukemia.»

That's when she, Shiffrin, started to cry, the ski racer recounted in 2015. She was tired and just wanted to go to bed. «And the little girl waited the whole time, just to be able to shake the winner's hand.» In January 2015, two young American skiers also died in an avalanche; one of them had gone to school with Shiffrin. All of this showed her how fleeting happiness can be.

In 2019, Shiffrin became world champion in slalom for the fourth time in Åre. At the press conference, she brought Emma Lundell onto the stage. No journalist had asked her about it, but it was important to her: «Emma, ​​you are my angel, my inspiration.» Lundell, then 17, had beaten cancer and become a competitive cross-country skier herself.

A year later, tragedy struck Shiffrin as well. Her father fell from the roof of their house while shoveling snow. The athlete had just won a race in Bansko when she received the news. She traveled home with her mother and lay down next to her father in the hospital bed. He died in her arms. The loss devastated her; it took Shiffrin almost two years to recover, both personally and athletically.

In an interview just over a year ago, she spoke about loss and grief, about the trauma she suffered, and about the mental emptiness it all triggered. In 2022, she began seeing a clinical psychologist. She said her primary goal wasn't to regain her full athletic performance. «We all have to learn when work takes priority, but also when life and love should be at the center,» she said. Shiffrin wanted to find her balance with the help of a professional.

The following winter, she won 14 races, surpassing Stenmark's record. She achieved this in Åre, Stenmark's hometown and the place where she first won, where she met Emma Lundell. It's fascinating how fate keeps intervening in this unusual career. Åre became a kind of emotional center for Shiffrin. Soon she'll return there with her 100th victory under her belt. The next race Shiffrin will enter is in Åre in just over two weeks.

The red carpet had already been rolled out in her homeland at the end of November for her 100th victory: on the East Coast of the USA, where she had lived since the age of eight and laid the foundation for all her successes on icy slopes. A triumph there would have had a special radiance. Shiffrin was leading in Killington after the first run of the giant slalom, but she crashed in the final and suffered a deep stab wound to her abdomen.

It took Shiffrin two months before she could return to the slopes. Impaling herself on her own ski pole triggered a trauma that still haunts her in giant slalom. On Saturday in Sestriere, Shiffrin missed qualifying for the second run.

## It has long since become a brand

Ultimately, Killington's injury was perhaps the decisive dramatic twist that transformed what seemed like an effortless string of victories into an existential struggle. This is likely to bring Shiffrin even more attention, especially back home, than she already enjoys. She already mentioned in our first extended conversation that she wants to establish herself as a brand. That's why she sometimes forgoes a summer vacation if it means, for example, a photoshoot with a major magazine.

The now 29-year-old therefore makes consistent use of social media. She posts pictures from the ski circuit, of her love for Norwegian ski racer Aleksander Kilde, of glamorous appearances, or of the Northern Lights in Levi.

But even on these platforms, she doesn't just remain a figurehead. She has campaigned for stricter gun laws in the US, and above all, she has shared her grieving process after her father's death with her followers. Many people who had also suffered tragedies thanked her for it. They told their own stories and wrote that, thanks to the athlete, they no longer felt alone in their grief.

Shiffrin seems to have found the perfect balance between work and life. This is evident in the way she overcame her injury and secured her 100th victory. With this achievement, a significant milestone has been reached, allowing the athlete to look to the future without any pressure. Where will the counter stand when she eventually retires? At 110? At 120? It will be a mark for the ages.
