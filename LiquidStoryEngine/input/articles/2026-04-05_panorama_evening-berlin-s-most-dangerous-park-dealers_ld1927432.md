# An evening in Berlin's most dangerous park: Dealers whisper, «Do you want marijuana?»

*Since March, Görlitzer Park in Kreuzberg has been closed off at night. Politicians hope this will make the park safer, but some residents are resisting.*

Görlitzer Park in Berlin-Kreuzberg is considered one of the most dangerous in Germany, but on this late Monday evening in March, it initially appears calm and peaceful. Cyclists and pedestrians crisscross the park. A man walks his dog, which wears a neon green collar. Soccer players train on a brightly lit sports field. It's a crisp evening. Only occasionally is there a faint whiff of cannabis in the air.

The dealers, however, are everywhere. They wear down jackets and baseball caps with the Louis Vuitton logo; they stand at the park entrances, lean against walls, or squat on huge rocks. «Hello. Everything alright?» they ask. «Do you want marijuana?» They are young men from Africa, and they also sell crack.

The residents don't seem to mind. They simply walk by. Some of them have gathered in the park this evening. They want their beloved «Görli» to remain open to everyone, especially at night.

Most are young, wearing black jeans and black hoodies. Some have pulled their hoods up. Grilled cheese sizzles over a fire, beer bottles clink together.

## «Pig cops, get out of Görli!»

Since the beginning of March, a security service has been locking all entrances to the park at 10 p.m., as was the case today; in the summer, the locks will be at 11 p.m. The aim of this measure is to curb drug dealing and crime in the park. In February, Senator Ute Bonde of the CDU party used drastic language to describe the measure. She said the park was no longer a place of recreation for «large segments of the population.» From now on, it will no longer be possible to enter the park at night; however, exiting through the turnstiles will always be permitted.

A few police officers walk past the demonstrators, shining their flashlights into the bushes. A beam of light flashes briefly, then disappears again. Then they position themselves at a safe distance from the young people. One of the officers shines his flashlight on the group. The people cheer, a man raises his arms. «Pig cops, get out of Görlitzer Park!» someone shouts, and the others join in. Someone throws a bottle at the police, which shatters on the asphalt path. In the darkness, it's impossible to see who threw it. The officers don't move.

A heated debate is raging over Görlitzer Park, affectionately known as «Görli» in Kreuzberg. Residents and activists have been protesting ever since the decision was made three years ago to close the park at night. A key argument is that the measure will only drive more drug dealers, addicts, and homeless people into the neighborhood. Several initiatives have formed, including «Görli 24/7,» «Rave against the Zaun,» and «Görli zaunfrei» (Görli Fence-Free). They consider the measure purely symbolic, if not an outright expression of authoritarian arbitrariness. Many don't believe the park is as dangerous as politicians claim. Are they right?

## In Kreuzberg, the heart beats left-green

The area around Görlitzer Park is one of the most vibrant parts of Kreuzberg. Many buildings date back to the Gründerzeit (late 19th-century boom period); their richly decorated stucco windows still bear witness to the heavy elegance of the German Empire. Today, nothing of that remains.

This district is the heart of a left-leaning, green lifestyle; the Green Party has held the mayor's office for 20 years. Restaurant menus feature vegan bento boxes, falafel, and spinach smoothies. Here, no one has to explain themselves if they receive welfare or wear shabby trousers. The Social Democratic Party (SPD) advertises «Housing without rip-offs» on posters, and the mosque on Falckensteinstrasse wishes everyone a blessed Ramadan. Walls and doors are covered in graffiti, and old clothes and a sink lie scattered beside the park.

In the late 1980s, local residents successfully campaigned to have the derelict site of the former Görlitz train station transformed into a park. Now it features playgrounds, barbecue areas, and a children's farm. But it also suffers from drug problems, neglect, and violence.

He speaks energetically at the chalkboard listing the daily specials. Then he walks away. On a street corner, a man smokes crack. The pipe glows in the darkness. A local resident recounts how drug addicts repeatedly shoot up between parked cars or sleep off their intoxication on the roadside. Homeless people sleep in doorways and relieve themselves there. Cars and basements are broken into, and syringes are thrown into bushes or next to playgrounds.

## Crack leads to rapid impoverishment

For about three years, crack cocaine, cooked with sodium bicarbonate, has flooded Berlin. It's a highly addictive drug that leads to rapid destitution. Since then, the situation has become particularly dire around Görlitzer Park. Not everyone can bear the misery. Some residents, especially those with children, have moved away.

> [embed: q-tool (chart/map) e182b63b2bd9fc0fd8e9cb783bbced54]

According to police crime statistics, in 2025 there were 550 assaults, 139 robberies, and 10 rapes or sexual assaults in the park and surrounding neighborhood. No other park in Berlin has such a high crime rate. Most of these incidents don't happen at night, but in broad daylight.

In the summer of 2023, a particularly horrific case shocked the city. Several men allegedly raped a woman in the park in front of her partner early one morning in June. The incident was reported by media outlets nationwide.

Berlin's Governing Mayor Kai Wegner subsequently convened a so-called security summit, at which the nighttime closure of the park was proposed. The Senate then implemented the plans despite resistance from the district.

Today, doubts exist as to whether the rape actually occurred as described. A video suggests that the sex may have been consensual; the court proceedings have been suspended. However, the politicians stuck to their plan: now, sixteen steel gates and eight turnstiles block the park entrances in the darkness, and sections of fencing fill the gaps.

## Residents: The fence is a complete waste of money

The city has indeed been trying to get the problems at Görlitzer Park under control for a long time. Zero-tolerance policies, park attendants and managers, a supervised injection site for addicts, designated areas for drug dealers, community caretakers, and weapons- and knife-free zones have all been tried. Many things have been attempted; nothing has worked.

If you listen to the conversations in the neighborhood, you'll find hardly any support for the new plan for the park. «Waste of money» is a word that comes up frequently, along with «nonsense» and «outrageous.» Aside from that, many in the neighborhood seem unfazed by the current situation. Of course, as a woman in the area, you have to be careful, says one young woman, but that's no different in other big cities. And what about the dealers? «They'd probably like to have a different job,» says a young man. How does he feel in the neighborhood? «Great.»

Only one woman seems exhausted by the situation. Jutta, 60 years old, has lived in the neighborhood for 40 years. She can be found near the park on a sunny afternoon. She points to a gas cartridge lying on the cobblestones. «Nitrous oxide, that's a huge thing here,» she says. Young people, in particular, inhale the gas from balloons. They become euphoric, almost in a trance.

Jutta says she feels safe during the day but sometimes has an uneasy feeling in the evening. She has experienced firsthand how the neighborhood has changed over the years. She recounts being attacked by an addict in April 2019. Shortly after she had unlocked her front door, she says, the man came up from behind and pushed her against the wall in the hallway. He ripped the necklace from her neck and fled, Jutta says, but neighbors caught him.

«How much more social work are we supposed to do?» Jutta asks. She loves city life but also needs security. Moving is out of the question for her because of her small pension.

## Activists file lawsuit

Meanwhile, the activists are serious about their protest. One morning, a man in dirty gray trousers and baggy shoes lounges in the sun amidst the ruins of the Pamukkale Well, a vast landscape of stone. Across the way, in front of a warehouse, activists from the «Görli zaunfrei» (Görli Fence-Free) alliance have set up parasols, beer tables, and wooden chairs. They have called a press conference to announce that they have filed a lawsuit and an urgent application against the park plans with the Berlin Administrative Court. Almost twenty residents have come.

David Werdermann is also there, the lawyer representing five plaintiffs. He's wearing a black turtleneck, an anthracite suit, black shoes, and horn-rimmed glasses. Werdermann sits behind a beer table and explains the residents' arguments into a microphone. Ultimately, he says, it's about defending public space against senseless bans and government intervention. «It's also a question of justice,» he says. After all, many Kreuzberg residents live in cramped conditions.

Next to Werdermann sit four of the plaintiffs; most have lived here for more than 25 years. Among them are Jürgen, who is blind and says that because the park closes at night, he has to take a detour in the evenings. Anna-Maria, who uses the park to escape from everyday life and emphasizes that many young people meet there. Mathias, for whom the park is an important place to cool off, especially in the summer, where he can have a beer with friends at night. And then there's Philipp, in black pants and a black hoodie. He lives in a dark building at the back without a balcony and uses the park for barbecues, birthday parties, and sports. «Without the park, something's just missing at night,» he says. He's sure there will be protests in the summer. «Then civil disobedience will take over.» Some of the residents applaud.

Kreuzberg is where you feel restricted in your freedom because you can't go to a park at night.

## Drug deals within seconds

Meanwhile, an alternative proposal to the nighttime park closures has been developed by the «Görli zaunfrei» (Görli fence-free) initiative. It is based on the so-called Zurich model , which considers the needs of the general public as well as those of drug users. In the 1990s, Zurich successfully used this model to largely eliminate the open drug scene in the city. Today, the model is considered a best practice example.

The plan for Kreuzberg includes another supervised consumption room for addicts, a crisis intervention team, more sanitary facilities and teams that are also on site at night. It is scheduled to be presented in May. What is missing, however, is the consistent criminal prosecution of drug trafficking that is part of the Zurich model.

But the dealers are experienced. If a police car approaches in the distance, they hop on a bicycle and ride off. The deals are also completed in the blink of an eye. On a broad daylight, three dealers are standing at the park entrance when two young men approach them. Words are exchanged, then one dealer goes to the trash can by the wall and takes out a package, while another stands watch. The seller fumbles with the package, the goods and the money change hands, and the two young men are off. A transaction in seconds, as if nothing had happened.
