# Thanks to this valley in the middle of the provinces, America rose to superpower status – today the place has an image problem. Billy Joel is partly to blame

*A visit to the Lehigh Valley, whose success story began with the defrauding of its Indigenous inhabitants.*

**Ivo Mijnssen, Bethlehem, Simon Tanner** · Gesellschaft · 2026-06-13 · 3652 words · [ld.10004841](https://www.nzz.ch/gesellschaft/american-dream-aus-stahl-wie-die-usa-zur-supermacht-wurden-ld.10004841)

This is the story of a promise: Those who work hard can achieve anything. In Allentown and Bethlehem, the twin cities in Pennsylvania, the American Dream became reality. Here, European adventurers found a valley brimming with legendary riches. The steel mill enabled ordinary workers to rise in society. They built skyscrapers and bridges that became world-famous, and the warships that transformed America into a superpower. The American bard Billy Joel sings in «Allentown» about what really mattered here, «what was real»: «Iron and coke, chromium steel.»

But the dream was shattered. Since then, the Lehigh Valley has come to symbolize the decline of American power and the end of a golden era. Joel sang of a gray, impoverished city where the unemployed kill time, without prospects, without hope. The image of the dying industrial city has stuck to Allentown to this day. Mayor Matt Tuerk can no longer bear to hear it. «Billy Joel should really pay our city compensation,» he says.

This is the story of a valley at the heart of the American dream. It represents the U.S. in microcosm: its rise, fall and reinvention. In the pioneer land of Pennsylvania, change always manifested itself particularly early and clearly. The new order here began with a fraud.

**I**Expansion

![](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/06/11/251052fa-1229-492e-832b-a68bf22d5ce1.jpg?crop=6000,4000,x0,y0)
** (Illustration by Simon Tanner / NZZ)

When entrepreneur William Allen first entered Pennsylvania’s Lehigh Valley, it was clear to him that if he wanted to develop the fertile land, the Indigenous people would have to go. The Lenape had lived there for millennia. But Allen, who had made a fortune as a shipowner trading rum, slaves and flour, saw the valley only as a «dense wilderness where the primitive red man roams and hunts, without the slightest sign of civilization.» In 1737, he sensed an opportunity to become even richer through a land deal. To do so, he had to defraud and expel the Lenape.

Allen was also chief justice of the colony of Pennsylvania. He summarily invented a purchase agreement: Decades earlier, the Native Americans had supposedly promised to sell the white settlers as much land as they could walk in 18 hours. Allen took the chiefs on leisurely walks to show them that no one could get very far in that time. They were familiar with the tradition of hostile parties meeting for a walk to reach an agreement. They agreed to the «Walking Purchase.»

But Allen sent not walkers but the fastest runners in Pennsylvania. «You’re running! That’s unfair, you were supposed to walk!» the Lenape shouted angrily from the path’s edge. They could only watch as the three athletes ran without a break, to the point of exhaustion. One runner gave up. Another collapsed and died shortly thereafter. Only Edward Marshall persevered, for 113 kilometers. He secured an area for the land speculator as large as the Swiss canton of Valais.

The settlers then forcibly drove out the Lenape. As payment, they received weapons and clothing, including 45 cannons, 2,000 needles and 40 mirrors. For decades, the Native Americans fought desperately for their homeland, even allying themselves with the French. «The land under my feet is the reason we are waging war against you,» said their chief, Teedyuscung. Chaos and violence reigned in Allen's promised land. Two decades after the Walking Purchase, he was still bitterly complaining about the poor security situation. His promise that this was «the best land in the world for the poor man, that very well suits the British character,» sounded like a mockery.

Larger settlements such as Bethlehem and Allentown developed slowly. They were subject to Lenape attacks until 1762. Just 15 years later, they became front-line towns in a new conflict: the American Revolutionary War. Allen's son James described how the army marched into present-day Allentown. «Thousands of wagons have passed my door, the hospitals are full.» With the armed forces came gunsmiths and transport entrepreneurs. The valley's primitive transport routes collapsed under the onslaught.

The Lehigh River could have solved the transportation problems. But it was a wild waterway full of sharp rocks and rapids. In the spring, it flooded large areas. For this reason, Americans began building a canal in 1818. Soon, timber and ore from the mountains could be shipped on it, tied together on rafts and boats, all the way to Philadelphia. Halfway there lay Bethlehem and Allentown. Refugees, German immigrants and adventurers settled here. Many were enterprising and well-educated. There were enough raw materials for the factories, and laborers as well. In 1857, the Saucona Iron Company built its first plant. This would become the Bethlehem Steel Corporation in the 20th century.

The company made a single product that shaped American modernity like no other: steel. And at its helm was an adventurer who realized his vision at great risk: Charles M. Schwab. The Pennsylvania native had worked his way up from engineer to director in Andrew Carnegie's steel mills. He believed in the American dream, in the ability of every person to fulfill their potential. «There are no limits to our personal development,» he was fond of saying. Schwab was a gifted salesman and made deals with major New York banks. When he had a falling out with J. P. Morgan, he left Carnegie's steel empire. In 1904, Schwab took over a small steel company in Bethlehem. It quickly became the second-largest producer in the U.S. Between the turn of the century and World War II, its production increased a hundredfold.

At Bethlehem Steel, Schwab staked everything on a novel, largely untested technology. When his secretary asked him if it was wise to risk the entire company on it, he said: «I have thought the whole thing through, and if we go broke, we'll go broke in a big way.» Bethlehem Steel began producing novel steel beams that were cheap and versatile, for use in skyscrapers, for example. Schwab's steel beams formed the skeletons of the most famous buildings on the New York skyline – including the Empire State Building. The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco was built with 44,000 tons of Bethlehem steel. Schwab's slogan was: «Steel built the world. Bethlehem built the steel.»

It was the age of American ascendancy, when cities grew into the sky and capitalism created unimaginable wealth. In his books, Schwab praised the morale of his workers: «I love to appeal to the American spirit in my men, the spirit of doing things better than anyone has done them before.»

The reality was considerably grimmer.

While workers received bonuses for exceeding their production targets, Schwab had them toil seven days a week, for 84 hours. Every year there were dozens of serious accidents: Workers were injured by glowing steel and inhaled toxic gases without protective equipment. Schwab did not tolerate unions. When workers went on strike in 1910 to demand Sundays off, he had mounted police fire into the crowd. People were killed and injured.

**II**War

![](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/05/12/6e742d8a-e03f-42e5-b061-a88a299927ec.jpg?crop=6000,4000,x0,y0)
** (Illustration by Simon Tanner / NZZ)

The first half of the 20th century was also a time of wars and crises. After the First World War, orders for the steel industry collapsed, and between 1929 and 1933 the Great Depression swept across the U.S. Building legendary bridges and luxurious skyscrapers was no longer a viable business model. Schwab recognized this early on: he reoriented Bethlehem Steel toward armaments production during wartime. In the First World War, the company not only supplied 100 ships and shipboard cannons, but also almost half of the artillery for the American army.

Schwab paid only limited attention to American laws. Although the U.S. was still neutral at the start of World War I, he negotiated a secret deal with the British to supply munitions and submarines. In the first months of the war, Bethlehem Steel delivered more than all British producers combined. Schwab was proud of this. «I can deliver anything if I get the money,» he reportedly said in London.

Schwab's deals caused controversy. Congressional committees accused him of exploiting his dominant position and charging Americans exorbitant prices. He was called a «merchant of death.» Schwab saw himself as being in the right. «I never had the feeling that we were doing anything other than our duty to the country and the world: to provide the means of defense.»

Schwab died in 1939, shortly after the start of World War II, an impoverished visionary. He had squandered his vast fortune on gambling, women and luxurious villas. And his shares in Bethlehem Steel were almost worthless: After 10 years of economic crisis, the company was on the verge of ruin. But with the U.S. entry into the war after Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, the company again became a central arms supplier. President Franklin D. Roosevelt called his country «the great arsenal of democracy.» Without steel from Pennsylvania, it could not have been filled. The industrial might of America stood on the front lines of a world war.

Bethlehem Steel expanded production and hired new workers. By 1943, the giant corporation had 300,000 employees working across America. They produced 73,000 tons of steel for the military and built more than 1,100 warships in the coastal shipyards. Among them was the aircraft carrier USS «Lexington,» which the Japanese destroyed in 1942 during the Battle of the Coral Sea. It sank with 200 men and 35 aircraft on board.

The enormous demand pushed the American steel industry to its limits. Because the men were in the army, there was an acute labor shortage. So women followed them into the factories. This was also true at Bethlehem Steel, where 25,000 women were suddenly soldering, welding and casting. For a conservative society, this was a shock that challenged gender roles. For the women, a new world opened up.

«The pay was very good for the time,» says former worker Doris M. Mann. «That’s why the women did it.» She was interviewed in 1975 for the «Women of Steel» research project. The Bethlehem native came from a poor family and had sick relatives. She needed the money. Mann initially tested the hardness of the steel that was to be used in fighter planes. Then she trained to be a crane operator. She liked the pressure, the responsibility: «Man, when you operate a crane, you have to be all there,» she said. The men watched her very closely.

The female workers also met with rejection. In Bethlehem, they underwent painful initiation rites. Men would leave hot pieces of metal on the welding torches and watch to see if the women cried when they removed them by hand. Sexual harassment also occurred. When Doris Mann was walking home from a night shift, a man exposed himself to her. «After that, my father walked with me.» She said workers regularly whistled at her. For her, that was normal.

In the U.S., wartime propaganda glorified the women in the factories. Fictional characters like Rosie the Riveter or Winnie the Welder appeared on large posters in Bethlehem and all over the country to recruit women. Rosie joined large pieces of steel with rivets; Winnie welded them together. The posters proclaiming «We Can Do It!» while Rosie flexed her muscles became world-famous. «She’s making history, working for victory. Watching out for sabotage,» a contemporary song says. The lyrics state that her boyfriend is fighting in the army, while «she’s doing overtime on the riveting machine.»

For worker Doris Mann, the job in the factory was a patriotic duty. But she also knew it could not last forever. «We went during the war because they needed women,» she says in the interview. «I think it showed that men and women can work together. I never thought of making a career out of it. Otherwise I wouldn’t have left.» She and the six million women in the arms industry were quickly laid off to make way for the returning soldiers.

**III**Decline

![](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/06/11/595e5b9b-6580-454f-b469-051474f9d145.jpg?crop=6000,4000,x0,y0)
** (Illustration by Simon Tanner / NZZ)

In 1945, the United States stood at the zenith of its power. It had made great sacrifices in World War II, but the country was not a landscape of ruins like Germany, Japan or the Soviet Union. The industries of its competitors were destroyed. In steel, the Americans had a virtual monopoly after the war. Their plants produced 90 tons a year. That was more than 60% of global production. An eighth of that came from Bethlehem.

Donald Young's career began in this golden age. «I come from the age of steam engines,» Young says on the phone. The 88-year-old belongs to the generation of Americans who secured their social mobility through hard work after the war. He joined Bethlehem Steel in 1956 and stayed for 56 years.Young was smart and ambitious. He took evening classes to continue his education, learning a great deal about metals and their processing. As a foreman, his pay increased. And the unions, which had become very influential in the factories since the war, secured him additional benefits. Young calls them «bennies»: health insurance, life insurance and a generous pension.

The man from Baltimore witnessed how rapidly technology developed. «When I started, we calculated the steel composition with a pen and paper,» he says. Now, he explains, computers do everything. And when he started, he adds, there was almost no safety equipment. «Now you have to wear an astronaut suit just to be allowed near liquid steel.» Young has little use for digitalization. He describes himself as an «old curmudgeon» who thinks back nostalgically to the old days. But he knows the steel industry perished because it did not change. And he remembers the exact day the decline began.

On July 15, 1959, a strike by 500,000 workers began that lasted four months. They demanded higher wages. Suddenly, supplies from the factories ceased – a shock for the construction sector, automakers and shipyards. The American government therefore allowed foreign steel into the country. The Europeans and Japanese were ready. «Suddenly, all the floodgates were open,» says Young. Overnight, the U.S. became a major steel importer.

The new competition hit Bethlehem Steel hard because its plants were outdated. «The technology was stuck at the level of the 1920s. For a long time, there was simply no reason to modernize,» says Don Young. The factories in Europe and Asia, on the other hand, were brand new. They produced more cheaply and efficiently, partly thanks to subsidies. Ironically, the Americans themselves had laid the groundwork for this: The first loans came from the Marshall Plan.

Bethlehem recovered once more. The global economy grew, and steel production reached a record high in 1973. Then came the oil price shock. Energy prices rose rapidly, making everything more expensive. At the same time, the auto industry, a key customer, collapsed. And because the government imposed new environmental regulations, the old plants now cost twice as much: They required more energy and had to be made cleaner at a cost of hundreds of millions of dollars. Bethlehem Steel incurred debt – just as interest rates were rising sharply. It was the perfect storm.

In 1977, the company reported a deficit of almost half a billion dollars. Five years later, this was followed by another $1.5 billion. At the time, it was the fourth-largest loss in American corporate history. The reasons lay in past mistakes, not least the generous pension benefits from the golden years. Bethlehem Steel laid off tens of thousands of workers and closed plants. The «Rust Belt» was born, a vast belt of shuttered, rusting industrial sites from Pennsylvania to the Midwest.

The steel industry was now in free fall. In Bethlehem itself, the wave of layoffs was spread out over a quarter of a century, as the company was hesitant to touch its most important plant. But in 1995, steel production ended in the Lehigh Valley as well. «The guys were crying when they shut down the blast furnaces. Me too,» says Don Young. «But I kept going.» When Bethlehem Steel went bankrupt in 2001, Young went on to work for another steel company. «I only retired when they forced me out at 75.» Today, he gives tours of the former plant grounds in Pennsylvania. In spirit, he remains a steelworker.

**IV**Emptiness

![](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/06/11/87af6907-ff39-4fa8-a3e8-1bf0dc55d931.jpg?crop=6000,4000,x0,y0)
** (Illustration by Simon Tanner / NZZ)

The deep crisis in the steel industry, a symbol of national strength, shook the entire country. Desolate landscapes and impoverished towns were left behind in the Rust Belt. In 1982, moved by the daily headlines about plant closures, Billy Joel wrote his song «Allentown.» It was actually about Bethlehem, but Joel felt that sounded too religious. Besides, Allentown rhymed better. «We’re living here in Allentown, and they’re closing all the factories down,» the New Yorker sang. «Out in Bethlehem they’re killing time. Filling out forms, standing in line.»

Joel wrote his requiem for the American dream. He meant the whole country when he spoke of Allentown, he says in a documentary. For the new generation, the promise of upward mobility and prosperity faded in the economic crisis of the 1980s. «Every kid had a pretty good shot to get at least as far as their old man got,» went another verse. But, the song continued, the children never made it.

In December 1982, at the height of the layoffs, Billy Joel performed in Bethlehem. The mayor of Allentown welcomed him and symbolically handed him the key to the city. «It's a raw song about a raw city,» he said during the ceremony . And: «Sure, we have unemployment and unfulfilled dreams, but who doesn't?» When Joel took to the stage that evening, the audience was agitated. A local journalist described the performance as a mixture of rock concert, workers' demonstration, and therapy session. «They took all the coal out of the ground. And the union men slunk away,» Joel sang. The workers cheered. «Don't take any shit from anybody,» he shouted to them. He played «Allentown» twice : at the beginning and at the end.

The decline of the steel industry changed the U.S. far beyond the Lehigh Valley, and politically as well. Ronald Reagan won the 1980 presidential election with the slogan «Make America Great Again.» He lamented the weakness of the U.S. – and idealized the postwar years as an era with a stable set of values, full of industrial potential.

In 2020, Donald Trump appeared in Allentown with the same message . He railed against «globalists» in Washington and the parasites in Europe who were merely exploiting America. Together, he claimed, they had destroyed industry. «Steel created the middle class, built our great skyscrapers, and won two world wars,» Trump said. He promised to revive industry with tariffs. The results of this policy were modest, but it resonated with many who felt overwhelmed by constant change and sought refuge in the past.

The abandoned factory site in Bethlehem today appears at first glance to be a typical industrial wasteland: an emptiness the size of Gibraltar. It's a depressing landscape with long halls full of shattered windows, a lost place. Rusting railway tracks covered in graffiti snake through former loading facilities. Behind them loom the disused blast furnaces.

**V** Reinvention

![](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2026/05/12/3ab13d39-fc81-4508-9f28-49f45fe8544f.jpg?crop=6000,4000,x0,y0)
** (Illustration by Simon Tanner / NZZ)

Only at second glance, on this cold winter day in late 2025, does it become apparent that there is life in the old walls. In a former workshop, the National Museum of Industrial History recounts the past. In the summer, concerts and festivals are held regularly next to it. One and a half million people visit the brownfield site every year. The television broadcaster PBS has moved into a new building, and a stadium is home to the local hockey team. Behind it stands a casino with a hotel and shopping center. Culture, shopping and entertainment: Bethlehem has reinvented itself for the American consumer society. Where workers once toiled, tourists now stand at slot machines or buy handmade jewelry at the Christmas market.

Industry hasn't disappeared from the Lehigh Valley. Allentown and the former Bethlehem Steel plant are part of a cluster of factories, warehouses, and shipping terminals. Companies like Amazon, FedEx, UPS, and Walmart have significantly expanded their presence. Brands like Olympus, Fender, and Crayola have remained. The transportation sector employs 40,000 people, almost the same number as Bethlehem Steel once did. While steel production is gone, the valley's proximity to the metropolises of New York and Philadelphia, its excellent transportation system, and the expertise of its residents remain its trump cards. This has changed little in the last 300 years.

Matt Tuerk, the mayor of Allentown, therefore dislikes hearing the term «Rust Belt.» «Billy Joel really ought to give back the key to the city,» he says in an interview. Tuerk is only half-joking when he demands compensation. As a former economic development officer, he says he had trouble attracting investors because they so strongly associated Allentown with industrial decline. The Democrat therefore prefers to emphasize the many new jobs and the large influx of people from other parts of the country.

The fact that Matt Tuerk became mayor at all shows how much the valley has changed. The 51-year-old is the first Latino in this position. His grandmother came to the US from Cuba after the war, and he grew up near the Lehigh Valley. Its inhabitants, including the steelworkers, were almost exclusively white back then. Now Allentown is a city with a Latino majority. Sixty percent of the residents have moved there since 2010. «They don't even know the steel era anymore. Because they weren't here,» says Tuerk.

But demographic change also creates uncertainty, Tuerk says. «There’s a segment of the long-established population that mourns the 1970s.» Politically, the Lehigh Valley is deeply divided. White suburban residents vote Republican, while Allentown and Bethlehem are in Democratic hands. In 2008, Obama won a majority in the valley. In 2024, it was Donald Trump. This too is related to demographic shifts: A portion of the Latino population is receptive to the conservative values of the American right. Added to this was dissatisfaction with the high cost of living and relatively low wages. This is a political development representative of the entire United States.

«There’s a lot of frustration in the city among those who aren’t benefiting from the growth,» says Mayor Matt Tuerk. This is closely linked to the changing economy: Many of the newcomers are leaving New York and other major cities because life has become too expensive for them. In Allentown, they find affordable housing because white residents left the city in the 1980s. The city centers became deserted.

But this development, too, is not permanent. Downtown Allentown is experiencing a tremendous boom. While simple stores with barred windows still stand in many places, one office tower after another is shooting up on the main street. Next to them are newly opened hotels and restaurants for a high-spending clientele. There is a modern technology museum for children, and the art museum is about to move into a new building. In the last five years, house prices have risen by two-thirds. Poor families fear they will be displaced.

The Lehigh Valley is thus on the verge of another transformation. What was once an undeveloped valley became the heart of American modernity, and a new economy was forged from an industrial wasteland. Indigenous tribes were followed by white Europeans and, more recently, Latinos. A multilayered and polarized society has emerged from this rich history. What still connects it is becoming increasingly uncertain. It is an emblem of the entire country, which is searching for a new place for itself in the world.

As Billy Joel sings: «Well we’re waiting here in Allentown. For the Pennsylvania we never found.» The valley and its people are reinventing themselves once again. What will come of it remains to be seen.
