# «Asians are no longer intimidated by the arrogance of Europeans.»

*Political scientist Kishore Mahbubani says the West is overwhelmed. The Americans are clinging to the illusion that they can still prevent China's rise. And the EU is completely disoriented. A discussion about the state of the world.*

He was a UN ambassador, president of the Security Council, dean of the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy – ​​and thinks like a philosopher. Kishore Mahbubani is one of Asia's most distinguished geopolitical intellectuals. He was born in India in 1948; his family later emigrated to Singapore. Mahbubani studied philosophy and, after graduating, began his diplomatic career. For over a decade, he served as Singapore's ambassador to the UN in New York.

In the interview, he challenges Europe. He speaks of a changing world order – and of the fact that the West still doesn't understand what's really going on. The greatest threat to Europe is not China, but its own self-righteousness.

**Mr. Mahbubani, the trade war between the US and China has escalated rapidly. Both sides are signaling maximum force. Who will back down first – Trump or Xi?**

The question is, who is better able to endure pain in the long run? I suspect it's the Chinese. The government in Beijing doesn't have to face any elections. The Republican Party, on the other hand, faces midterm elections in two years. And the worst-case scenario for Donald Trump is if the Republicans lose in 2026. This situation weakens Trump's position.

**So you consider democracy a disadvantage in a trade war?**

Not only that. China is a huge country and largely self-sufficient in many areas. That's why it can withstand enormous pressure.

**At the same time, China's government is also under pressure because the economy is already in crisis.**

When it comes to China's economy, the West thinks in terms of one, two, or three years. The Chinese, on the other hand, think in terms of decades. If we had conducted this interview ten years ago and I had said, «Give China another ten years, and it will seriously compete with Mercedes, BMW, or Toyota»—you would have laughed at me. And what happened?

**Electric cars from China have become so good that even the EU has imposed import tariffs on them to protect European car companies. Are you suggesting that China's economy is only facing temporary difficulties?**

Yes. Currently, the mood among Chinese entrepreneurs and consumers is subdued. But that can change quickly. Whether it's solar panels, wind turbines, battery technologies, electric vehicles, or robotics: China invests more in future-oriented industries than any other leading economy. The fruits of these investments will come.

**When historians look back on this moment in history in the future, will they mark Trump as a turning point in the decline of the West and the rise of Asia?**

The rise of Asia and the relative decline of the West are not short-term phenomena. They are driven by major historical forces. We are returning to the normality of world history. The last two hundred years of Western dominance appear to the West as a matter of course. In reality, they were the exception. For almost two millennia – from the year 1 until around 1800 – China and India were consistently the two largest economies in the world. The West then gained ground with the Enlightenment, the Renaissance, and the Industrial Revolution. But it was always only a matter of time before Asia caught up – and that is precisely what is happening now.

**When will Asia overtake the West?**

By 2050, China and India could once again be the world's number one and two economies. And this will happen regardless of whether Trump or Biden is in the White House. These tectonic shifts are like an approaching wave: unstoppable.

**You speak of a «relative loss of importance» of the West. What exactly do you mean by that?**

Relatively speaking, because Western countries can also be successful in the Asian century, provided they understand that geopolitical conditions are changing and adapt accordingly. So far, the record is sobering. America and the European Union are geopolitically overwhelmed in different ways. America is clinging to the illusion that it can still prevent China's rise. The European Union seems genuinely disoriented. The continent needs a complete strategic reboot.

**What leads you to that conclusion?**

Europe baffles me. The most obvious example is the war in Ukraine: it could entirely have been avoided, from the very beginning. Europe simply had to seriously address Russia's deep-seated security interests. When NATO declared in 2008 that Ukraine should join the defense alliance, it was an enormous provocation for Russia. Why do they do such a thing? It demonstrates a complete lack of common sense. In October 2022, I spoke with Henry Kissinger, and he told me he could hardly comprehend what had happened to Europe. A hundred years ago, Europe ruled the world—today, it isn't even capable of governing itself.

**They imply that Europe provoked this war.**

If you go into a bear's den and poke it in the eyes, you shouldn't be surprised if it strikes back. But the European stands there astonished and asks: Why did the bear hurt me?

**From a European perspective, Russia is the aggressor.**

This shows how naive Europe has become. Anyone who still doesn't recognize that the West bears some responsibility for this chaos is deluding themselves. Of course, the Russian invasion of Ukraine is illegal, a clear violation of international law. But when the West imposed sanctions on Russia, they were supported by only about 15 percent of the world's population. 85 percent didn't. This demonstrates how isolated and out of touch the European perspective is.

**How should Europe react when confronted with a country like Russia that pursues imperialist goals and attacks neighboring countries?**

I say this as a friend of Europe: Europe should ask itself how to live with difficult neighbors. Why don't you try to understand that Russia sees the world completely differently? It's different in Asia. We are extremely diverse, but we get along – precisely because we know how different we are. We don't expect others to become like us. Europe, on the other hand, has this idealistic zeal that everyone should become like them.

**You're turning a war into a diversity debate. Ukraine didn't want to change Russia, it simply wanted independence.**

I stand by my point: the real question is this: Can you handle diversity – in a world where you are a minority? Europeans now make up only 6 to 7 percent of the world's population. And even if you count the entire Western world, it's barely more than 12 percent. 88 percent live outside the West – and it is precisely these 88 percent who are now beginning to wake up. The question is: Do you want to adapt to this new world – or do you really expect it to adapt to you?

**The Asian countries get along far less well with each other than you suggest.**

We are not at war.

**China has had border disputes with India for years. In the South China Sea, China claims territories that near-neighboring states also claim. There are also tensions with Japan and South Korea.**

You mentioned India: Do you know who India's most important trading partner is? It's China. Of course, there are tensions, yes – but they manage, they find solutions, they maintain perfectly normal economic relations. When will Europe resume normal trade relations with Russia?

**They accuse Europeans of being naive. What do you see as the cause?**

Europe boasts excellent universities and renowned think tanks – and yet it seems stuck in a 19th- and 20th-century way of thinking. This isn't just about colonial patterns of thought, but about a deeply ingrained sense of superiority. And many Europeans still carry this arrogance with them today.

**Do you experience this feeling of superiority primarily in political action or also in everyday life?**

There too, but the crucial point is: Asians are no longer intimidated by this. It used to be different. In my memoirs, I describe growing up in a British colony—I was born in 1948, and Singapore remained under British rule until 1963. For the first fifteen years of my life, I genuinely believed that as an Asian with brown skin, I was inferior to the white man—that he was more intelligent, more capable, simply better. For me, that was the natural order of things. It took me a long time to realize: That's not true.

**When did you begin to question this worldview?**

This was a slow process – fueled by the rise of Asia. Let me give you a simple figure to illustrate how much the world has changed: in 1980, the EU's gross domestic product was ten times that of China. Today, they are roughly equal. And by 2050, the EU will be only half the size of China. So, if you shrink from ten times to half the size – and yet continue to talk down to China – the problem isn't with China, it's with yourself.

**Do you think history could reverse itself – and Asians could feel superior in the future?**

No, I don't think so. But they will no longer feel inferior either. Europeans often speak condescendingly about the rest of the world—without realizing it. They make demands, impose sanctions, and take it upon themselves to judge others. But that old power of interpretation is gone.

**If you are right, and China becomes the largest economy and India the second largest by 2050: What would this mean for the world?**

When a major power rises, the question arises: Do you want to provoke it – or work with it? Understanding China doesn't mean submitting to it. The Vietnamese have a clever saying for this: Whoever wants to lead Vietnam must simultaneously be able to stand up to China – and get along with China. This requires geopolitical skill and strategic finesse. And that, I fear, is precisely what Europe has lost.

**They advise Europe to play a more independent role – including vis-à-vis the United States. Do you believe that this is truly in Europe's interest? Or does it ultimately serve primarily China?**

Experienced European statesmen once said: States have neither lasting friends nor lasting enemies – they only have lasting interests. During the Cold War, the interests of Europe and the USA coincided because they faced a common adversary: ​​the Soviet Union. But in twenty or thirty years, Europe's greatest challenge will no longer come from Russia.

**But rather?**

Africa's demographic development is Europe's biggest future issue. In 1950, Europe had twice as many inhabitants as Africa. Today, Africa's population is two and a half times that of Europe. By 2100, it will be ten times larger. If Africa doesn't develop, its people will migrate to Europe. And we are already seeing the political consequences of a few thousand refugees: far-right parties are gaining strength, the AfD is becoming increasingly influential, and the extreme right is in power in Italy. Therefore, Europe's strategic goal should be to enable as much economic development as possible in Africa. China is currently investing the most in Africa. It's understandable that the US wants to contain China. But why should it be in Europe's interest to simply follow suit?

**China's close ties to Russia do not exactly inspire confidence in Europe. Why doesn't China use its influence on Russia to persuade Putin to stop his war?**

The Chinese are intelligent and strategically astute. China doesn't want to dominate the world – governing its own country is challenge enough. So why would China voluntarily entangle itself in conflicts abroad? In the 15th century, the Chinese fleet was far superior to the Spanish and Portuguese. China could have conquered the world back then. But it didn't, because that's not part of its cultural DNA.

**China is indeed deeply involved in the war in Ukraine as a supplier of war-related goods to Russia. The country is massively rearming. Chinese companies operate a global port network with over a hundred locations – on almost every continent. And with the Belt and Road Initiative, China has a presence in numerous countries. All with peaceful intentions?**

China's global influence will continue to grow. That's a reality. But will China therefore conquer other countries? Certainly not. Although it would be capable of doing so. Western great-power thinking is alien to China. China doesn't behave like the United States. Imagine if China behaved like Donald Trump – and simply demanded: Give us back Greenland.

**What about Taiwan? China's intention to annex the country sooner or later is obvious.**

The best way to secure peace in Taiwan is quite simple: don't change the status quo. As long as Taiwan doesn't declare independence, there will be no war. It's that simple. Those who want to change this status quo are the troublemakers. For example, the visit of influential US politician Nancy Pelosi to Taiwan in 2022 was completely unnecessary. The US officially adheres to the policy that Taiwan belongs to China, the so-called One-China policy. Then they should act accordingly.

**Many people in the West view China through the same lens they once used to view the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union was clearly expansionist; that was part of its communist ideology. You, on the other hand, believe that China has completely abandoned this goal—why are you so certain of that?**

Let me ask a very simple question: When Xi Jinping goes to bed at night, do you seriously believe he dreams of Marx and Lenin? Chinese civilization has existed for five thousand years. And what did Chinese emperors dream of for centuries? Stability and prosperity for their civilization. Xi Jinping wants to make Chinese civilization great again. And if you think he wants to make communism great again, then you've clearly read too much American propaganda.

**Why are all party documents still full of Marxist-Leninist vocabulary? Is it just folklore?**

In geopolitics, one must learn a crucial lesson: Don't take things literally—take them seriously. Try to understand people's thinking. You have to delve deeper, into their psyche, into their history. I encourage Europeans: Learn! Strive to improve! Even if China were truly terrible, a genuine threat to its neighbors, the question remains: What European interests are being served today by antagonizing China? That's what I'd like to understand.

**China is simultaneously a partner, competitor, and systemic rival for Europe – democracy on the one hand, autocracy on the other. The threat lies in the fact that an authoritarian regime is in power in China, and that this regime is gaining influence worldwide.**

Are you seriously telling me that it is Europe's job as a democracy to export democracy?

**It's not about exporting – it's about preserving democracy.**

Nobody will take your democracy away from you – certainly not the Chinese.

******What will China's political system look like in 2050? Do you see any trends towards democracy?**

The idea that Europe can teach China democracy – anyone who truly believes that is utterly naive. Do you seriously think Europe can teach China anything? Ultimately, everything lies in the hands of the Chinese people. A country's cultural DNA doesn't change overnight. If China has a bad ruler, it loses its Mandate of Heaven. If it has a good ruler, the people are content. That's how it's been for millennia – and that's how it will continue. And I urge you on one point: never underestimate the complexity of Chinese thought. In many ways, it's more nuanced and sophisticated than European thought – which often gets stuck in black-and-white categories: democracy or autocracy, friend or foe.

**Give an example.**

You only have to look at what China is doing. When China launched the Belt and Road Initiative, the US started a global counter-campaign. They urged countries not to participate. 193 countries had the choice – 150 countries joined. Isn't that a remarkable achievement by the Chinese? Do you think it just happened by chance – or perhaps it's the result of a highly complex strategy?

**The rise of Asia – and especially China – was only possible because it was integrated by the West.**

Yes, of course – China has benefited enormously from the Western-dominated global trading system. And that is precisely why China wants to preserve the rules-based world order of 1945. While the US is withdrawing from the World Trade Organization, China remains firmly entrenched within it. In fact, China's commitment to globalization is deeper today than that of any other country in the world.

**How do you see the future of globalization – against the backdrop of the current trade war?**

The good news is that almost all Asian countries are pushing globalization forward – and we hope that Europe will follow suit. This is an opportunity for Europe and Asia to stand together for globalization and actively defend it.

******Do you see no danger that a trade war between the US and China could ultimately escalate into a real war?**

No, I don't believe that. All the major players are aware of the danger and are acting accordingly. Of course, many in Asia are worried about Trump and his tariffs – but nobody believes that this will change the long-term direction of world history.

**They remain calm.**

You know, I came from extreme poverty – from rock bottom, literally. When I was six, I was put on a nutrition program because I was malnourished. We lived without a flush toilet, debt collectors came to the door, my father went to prison. I've experienced real Third World poverty. And you want to tell me that something even worse could happen? No way.__ If you don't understand where Asian countries come from, you will never understand their resilience.
