Everything happens very quickly. Shortly after my interview request via email, Dayanita Singh wants to FaceTime. Her cheerful face appears on the screen, and the unapproachable image I had of the internationally renowned photographic artist immediately dissolves. This warm exchange continues until our meeting.

One of the first terms that comes up in conversation with Dayanita Singh is «dissemination.» «Dissemination,» that's what she's aiming for, she says. Although the German word doesn't fully capture the meaning. Dayanita Singh wants to make art accessible to as many people as possible, for example through books.

The book is a democratic means of disseminating art – a book is more affordable than a painting or a sculpture. Singh's sales events are also significant in this context. Each book is signed by her and lovingly wrapped in fabric. Anyone who buys one is simultaneously treated to an encounter with her and ennobled as a curator. Anyone who owns such a work can easily put together their own Dayanita Singh exhibition. The artist's ideas spread.

But «dissemination» also includes the idea of ​​germination; the word derives from the Latin verb «disseminare,» meaning to sow. It originates from the world of plants. By spreading an idea, one changes one's environment.

I first met Dayanita Singh a few weeks later in Italy, more precisely in Bologna. There, the Indian-born artist held a small ceremony at the State Archives. She presented the «Archivio di Stato di Bologna» with one of her «pillars»—as she calls them: stacked wooden structures covered with her photographs, resembling columns.

With this gift, she expresses her gratitude for being allowed to photograph in the archives of the capital of Emilia-Romagna. The small-scale gift ceremony has the feel of a performance. With an almost dance-like movement, the artist removes the plastic covering that concealed the artwork.

She seems delighted, and everyone present applauds. But shortly afterward, her critical gaze reveals that she wants to change something about the composition of the pictures. Two friends restack the heavy wooden pieces until it looks right to her.

## Study in New York

Dayanita Singh is internationally renowned for her artist's books, which she conceives of as portable museums. A distinctive feature of her work is how she presents photography, thereby expanding the boundaries of the medium. She typically displays her black-and-white photographs of people—friends, family, musicians, or dancers—archives, or buildings in mobile, self-designed wooden structures.

There are two distinct schools of thought on how to approach an artist's work: either starting with her biography or with her work itself. In Dayanita Singh's case, both approaches are equally insightful. Her very first publication, from 1986, was formative for her life and work. The book was titled «Zakir Hussain,» after the legendary tabla player and composer (1951–2024) who played a central role in Dayanita's development.

The tabla is an Indian percussion instrument whose rhythmic sounds possess a trance-like quality. Singh translated this musical technique into a book by collaged the grid-like image sequences of contact prints and combining them with handwritten notes. The book was published while she was still studying at the National Institute of Design (NID) in Ahmedabad, where she studied visual communication.

For six winters, the young woman accompanied Hussain's troupe and toured India with the musicians. Although the group played traditional Indian music for a wide audience, they had come into contact with Western music at an early stage.

As a teenager, Hussain met Beatles member George Harrison, who had traveled to India to study with sitar master Ravi Shankar. During their tour with Hussain, Singh not only photographed his performances but also the musicians' daily lives.

Focusing on a single figure and documenting their life was unusual in photography at the time. Later, she decided to train as a photojournalist and documentary photographer in New York. She learned to focus from Zakir Hussain – because when playing, a musician must be completely present with their instrument and able to block everything else out. Focus isn't just important in photography, she says; it's also central to her life as an artist. His concept of dissemination also influenced her. After all, that's precisely what the musician did: he disseminated classical Indian music.

Singh grew up as the eldest of four sisters in a well-to-do environment marked by transformations in several respects: After the partition of India in 1947, her parents had to move from Lahore to Delhi. Her father, she tells us during our second meeting in her Delhi studio, was obsessed with constantly rearranging their apartment. When guests came, he would transform the living room into a bedroom. He was also constantly rearranging furniture.

Her father's constant reconfiguration of the home greatly displeased her mother. By nature, she was an archivist, a woman who craved order. However, one thing the parents did share was a sense of curation.

While Kanwar Mahinderpal Singh curated the living spaces, Nony Singh, the mother, used the glass table as a display case for her photographs. Or she compiled extensive photo albums. And who was her absolute favorite subject?

Exactly, Dayanita Singh, how could it be otherwise? And what became of this daughter? An artist who constantly rearranges her own photographs.

Dayanita Singh likes to compare photography to raw vegetables. Photography, too, is raw material that needs to be transformed. She doesn't simply photograph beautiful things. Her images, whether of people or inanimate objects, are always traces of relationships. She captures moments of togetherness.

Singh speaks of photography as a form of friendship. One of the questions she poses is: How can photography become a physical and emotional experience? In this context, the Indian artist also speaks of photography as a hyperlink.

Singh's paintings are rarely intended to be hung on walls. They are either published in books or arranged in groups within wooden structures for exhibitions. Sometimes these structures resemble shipping crates on wheels, sometimes oversized screens, and occasionally almost houses.

![One of the nine leporellos in the Bhavan Museum. The book transforms into an exhibition in front of the Taj Mahal.](https://img-msp-prod.nzz.ch/2025/10/11/085ea835-134b-4788-85d2-b824989bc36e.jpg?width=1024)

This type of presentation is significant in several respects. Firstly, it recalls the father's activity as a «family architect.» A family friend was a well-known architect and designer and had his furniture made by the same carpenter who also worked for Singh's family.

Singh still has her precisely crafted displays made of dark teak wood produced in this family workshop. Furthermore, these spatial structures challenge the classical form of photography by bringing it closer to installations, as seen in contemporary art since the 1970s.

A short time later, Dayanita Singh and I met again in India. Following the itinerary of several exhibitions initiated by the artist, I traveled from Mumbai to Ahmedabad and from there to Vadodara.

It is, as it were, the artist's eyes that guide my journey through «her» country. I visit many impressive temples and buildings. Even more significant, however, are the encounters with local people that she facilitates for me. I am not simply a tourist, but a visitor. Through the artist, I become part of her network.

## The «third breast»

Arriving in Delhi, the journey takes less time than expected because it's Sunday. I'm on my way to the artist's studio. Delhi is a city with around 34.7 million inhabitants, and navigating this behemoth is an unpredictable undertaking.

I arrive a little early, and the artist is still in her daily yoga session. In India, yoga isn't a lifestyle activity for stress management, but rather a part of everyday life. Similarly, photography is Singh's everyday way of seeing the world. It's fitting, then, that she refers to her camera, which she often carries at waist level, as her third breast. Perhaps this also explains her grounded demeanor and open nature.

Before Singh shows me her studio, which is located above her apartment, she makes an unusual suggestion: would we like to spontaneously curate a mini-exhibition together?

She takes out a box containing 16 objects designed by a friend who is an architect: a kind of nut, a bird, coins – and unusually – a totem with a human head – all painted gold.

The collection is embedded in a layer of material reminiscent of earth. When you pick up the artifacts, an imprint remains. Less than twenty minutes later, Singh looks at the result with satisfaction: the resulting arrangement could serve as a model for a future exhibition. This is definitely an unusual way to begin an interview. And it offers insight into the artist's working method.

Dayanita Singh's studio is a magical place: a paradise of books and a museum in one. Over the course of her career, Singh has created various «mobile museums» dedicated to different themes: archives, architecture, music, or portraits of people. Nine of these series were brought together in the 2017 book «Museum Bhavan.» The artist's museums continue to grow to this day.

Through her practice, she turns the Western concept of the museum as a lofty temple of art on its head. And sometimes Singh herself becomes a walking museum: In «My Life as Museum» (2018), she wore a jacket with nine pockets in Venice, each containing one of her «Museum Bhavan» leporellos. Everything is in motion, just as it was when she toured India with Zakir Hussain.

Several stacks of photographs lie on a table. Dayanita picks up a few pictures, and suddenly a detail catches her eye. She holds two pictures side by side: «What's going on here? I know something's happening,» she says.

As the archivist of her own archive, she can respond to existing material in new ways, creating new lines and narratives. Archives as places of hidden and silenced knowledge have long fascinated Singh; she has been photographing these spaces for many years in both India and Italy. Next year, in mid-April, a major exhibition will open at the «Archivio di Stato» in Venice, showcasing her 25-year engagement with Italian archives.

Her own photographic archive is becoming increasingly important to her. She has been working intensively with it since the pandemic. The first «Montages,» in which she reconfigures old and newer images, were created starting in 2019.

She cuts her photographs herself, overlays multiple images, and creates something new by layering widely separated eras and places: echo chambers that bridge time and space. As a viewer, one senses that different worlds are meeting here, yet the eye accepts these fictional places as third spaces.

In doing so, Singh also exposes the constructed nature of every photograph – a highly topical issue, keyword AI-manipulated images. She says that these superimpositions have made her a «photo-architect.»

## Singh's Paths to Switzerland

She repeatedly mentions mentors who significantly changed her life. Zakir Hussain was one such person. Another figure is Mona Ahmed, a transgender person. Singh met Ahmed in 1989 while working as a photojournalist; she was tasked with portraying a eunuch community in Delhi.

The pictures were not initially published; instead, a close friendship developed from this encounter. And this is where Zurich comes into play, or rather, the publisher Walter Keller (1953–2014). Singh first met him in 1992 at the bookstore of his publishing house, Scalo, located on Weinbergstrasse.

He looked at the portfolio of the then 31-year-old photographer and recommended that she not make books initially, but instead focus on building an archive. Dayanita Singh followed this advice.

In 1997, she received a grant from the foundation established by Swiss photographer Robert Frank, which allowed her to continue her career as a photographer. At that time, Singh worked primarily as a family photographer; she gave the portraits to the families as gifts. When Walter Keller agreed to publish her photographs, the next surprise followed.

He wanted to show Dayanita's pictures of Mona. Of course, not without her consent. The book launch took place at the Mehendyan Cemetery in Delhi, where Mona lived until her death in 2017. Even the Swiss ambassador attended. The video of the book launch was shown this year in the exhibition «Mona and Myself» at the Gallery White in Vadodara.

I looked at them. Even after her death, Mona haunts several of the artist's photomontages, almost as a ghost. In one video, Mona can be seen reciting old poems in Urdu.

As soon as I stepped out of the air-conditioned gallery space in Delhi, I was enveloped by the Indian heat. This sensation mingled with what I had seen and heard. Photography is also about feeling. That is the gift Dayanita Singh gives us.
