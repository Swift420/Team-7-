# The old world order has collapsed. Europe must adapt to chaos in order to survive.

*For over three decades, Europe believed that war was a thing of the past. Now it's back, and it's clear: rules only apply as long as an authority is willing to enforce them.*

The world order now appears somewhat less chaotic than it did two or three weeks ago: Israel and Iran have a ceasefire, Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo have signed a peace agreement, and US President Trump reaffirmed American security guarantees for Europe at the NATO summit in Brussels.

Some wars and conflicts that recently filled us with fear and terror have been temporarily halted. But this is by no means the case for all wars and conflicts: Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine continues unabated, and no one can say when or how it will end; the war in the Gaza Strip is also not over, and the civil wars in Libya and Sudan persist. These receive little attention in Europe, even though they are one of the causes of migration.

That the Iranian nuclear program has actually been set back by years seems questionable after the statements made by US intelligence agencies. Donald Trump's initial grand pronouncements were clearly exaggerated. He has since indirectly admitted this himself when he threatened to order further airstrikes if the Iranian regime did not definitively abandon the development of nuclear weapons and delivery systems. The recent lull in the chaos of world politics is therefore likely to be little more than a temporary reprieve. That is, if it is indeed a reprieve at all, and not a respite before the next exchange of blows.

## Sanctions instead of bombs

But why has there been such a recent increase in wars? In Europe, for over three decades, we had held the expectation that interstate war was a thing of the past, that military force would be replaced by economic power as the mode of steering international politics. We believed that armed force would increasingly be replaced by economic sanctions when it came to bringing a rule-breaker and disruptor of order to heel, and that international arbitration tribunals would take over the role of military strikes and resolve conflicts peacefully.

We were convinced that interstate wars would then cease to exist, and that in the case of intra-societal wars, the international community's responsibility to protect would come into play, bringing an end to civil wars fought in the form of massacres. Europeans saw themselves on the path to a world in which it would be possible to «create peace with fewer and fewer weapons.»

What has replaced this is not only an increase in wars, but also the need to secure peace through the provision of an ever-growing quantity of weapons. The prospect of a stable and cost-effective peace has vanished and will not return in the foreseeable future.

## Power in the service of order

What went wrong? Yes, there are a number of decisions that, in hindsight, could have been made differently. But whether that would truly have led to us continuing to live in a peaceful world, to the Russians not attacking Ukraine, and to Hamas not invading Israel, is doubtful.

It wasn't contingent decisions, but rather structural problems within the rules-based order that led to its erosion. The US has lost political authority: The US president had to use B-2 bombers against Iran to persuade both sides in Tehran (and Tel Aviv) to agree to a ceasefire. It took military force to establish authority. And it is highly questionable whether authority gained in this way will last long, or whether it will require – again and again – a demonstration of military superiority.

The authority of a guardian of the world order is the prerequisite for political actors to comply with the rules without having to resort to force. The fundamental conditions for peace without the use of force have never been sufficiently clarified; therefore, the rules-based order has always been subject to the proviso that there must be an irresistible actor who places their power at the service of this order. Where this was not the case, the order stood on shaky ground and threatened to collapse as soon as someone intervened with great force. This was the case at the end of February 2022 with Russia's war of aggression against Ukraine.

## Magical thinking

But who was the guardian of this order—or at least could have been? Many Europeans placed their trust in the United Nations, which was legitimately positioned to guarantee the rules. But the UN was almost always blocked by the veto of the permanent members of the Security Council whenever existential questions were at stake for a veto-wielding power. The world organization was and is a weak guardian when things get serious. Other Europeans trusted the USA, which had sufficient power to enforce compliance against rule-breakers—except in the case of the nuclear powers Russia and China.

However, the US had a fatal tendency to confuse the role of guardian with that of master, prioritizing its own interests over the rules. This led to the formation of an anti-American coalition led by Russia and China, which included virtually the entire Global South.

This became relevant when the UN General Assembly was to condemn Russia for its attack on Ukraine and impose sanctions, and those mentioned voted against it or abstained. It was, in effect, half of humanity that abstained or voted against imposing sanctions on the rule-breaker. With that, the rules-based order was finished, even if some politicians refuse to acknowledge it and believe they can salvage the rule-based structure with incantations. But that's magical thinking, which has never helped.

## No silver lining on the horizon

Furthermore, the US withdrew from its role as guardian of global peace, first gradually and then dramatically, for entirely understandable reasons. Global compliance with the rules and the peace built upon it are a collective good from which no one can be excluded, even if that individual contributes absolutely nothing to its availability.

The US, in its role as guardian, had to contribute the lion's share, leading both its elites and its voters to question whether this wasn't ultimately an unfair disadvantage. Donald Trump's «America First» policy represented a resounding abandonment of the guardian role and a decisive shift towards its own national interests—even if one sometimes gets the impression that Trump isn't entirely sure what those interests are. For the global order, however, the only relevant point is that he isn't interested in the well-being of others and disregards the rules when they prevent him from pursuing his own interests.

This gave the rule-breakers carte blanche, and the USA firmly established itself among them. From the cosmos of rules-based order thus arose the chaos of international politics in which we currently find ourselves. There is no silver lining on the horizon heralding the arrival of a new order. Europeans, in particular, will have to adapt to a chaotic order in order to assert themselves. If they fail to do so, they will suffer structural disadvantages.

## A lesson in power politics

The implications of this were recently demonstrated by efforts to persuade Iran to negotiate the end of its nuclear program. The Europeans lacked the coercive measures to motivate the Iranian regime to negotiate, and thus were stalled and unable to achieve anything. Israel and the US, on the other hand, possess these measures and used them decisively.

In a power-based order, superior air strikes are an alternative to merely offering negotiations, at least as a means of forcing them. This was a lesson in power politics, demonstrating to Europeans how to end fighting and compel negotiations.

We must prepare ourselves for the fact that this kind of behavior will set a precedent and that those who merely point to the rules will politically lose out. Where chaos reigns, one needs the means to assert oneself within it and to enforce a minimum of adherence to rules. The current moment of respite gives us the opportunity to reflect on the new circumstances and reposition ourselves. However, this «moment of respite» means that we don't have unlimited time. Europe must act quickly.
