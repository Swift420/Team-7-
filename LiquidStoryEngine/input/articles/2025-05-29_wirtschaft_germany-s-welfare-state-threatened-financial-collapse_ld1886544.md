# Germany's welfare state is threatened with financial collapse

Lars Klingbeil has just under four weeks left to deliver his final project. On June 25, the Federal Cabinet is scheduled to approve the 2025 budget, for which Klingbeil, as Finance Minister, is responsible. The projected gap between revenues and expenditures is expected to be larger than it has been in a long time. According to sources within the CDU/CSU, expenditures could exceed revenues by up to €100 billion. This would make the deficit almost twice as large as the one projected by the previous coalition government before it collapsed over the budget dispute .

One reason for the deep deficit is the rapidly rising expenditure in social security, particularly in health and long-term care insurance. To prevent social security contributions from rising even faster, the government intends to provide financial support from the federal budget. A €10 billion injection of funds for statutory health insurance and €6 billion for long-term care insurance is under discussion.

The government has just transferred an early federal subsidy of €800 million to the health fund, from which statutory health insurance funds receive their money, to secure the fund's liquidity. Further funds are needed, says Health Minister Nina Warken. The financial situation of the statutory health insurance system, which incurred a deficit of €6 billion in 2024, is «more dramatic than previously assumed.» The system is «deep in the red.»

Doris Pfeiffer, the chairwoman of the board of the National Association of Statutory Health Insurance Funds (GKV), is therefore calling for immediate action. «Otherwise, health insurance premiums will skyrocket at the next turn of the year,» she warns. Pfeiffer advocates a spending moratorium for all areas of healthcare. «No more price or fee increases that exceed current revenues,» she says, until reforms bring revenues and expenditures back into balance.

## Subsidies from the federal budget

The financial situation of Germany's social security system is precarious. This isn't limited to health and long-term care. In the pension and unemployment insurance systems, too, expenditures are threatening to outpace revenues. Meanwhile, employees and their employers are already paying more money into the system than at any time since World War II. Nearly 42 percent of gross income currently flows into social security in the form of contributions.

Without sweeping reforms, contributions will continue to rise rapidly in the coming years due to demographic trends. They drive up non-wage labor costs, jeopardize jobs, reduce workers' purchasing power, and stifle growth. The welfare state risks destroying its own foundations.

But instead of tackling the problems through structural reforms, the federal government is papering them over with subsidies from the federal budget, thus driving national debt even higher.

## Expansion of the welfare state

The massive expansion of the welfare state in Germany began in the early 1970s under the Social Democratic Chancellor Willy Brandt. At that time, employees and employers together only had to pay 26.5 percent of gross income into social security. The rapid expansion of social benefits caused contributions to rise sharply in the following years. By the early 1980s, the total contribution had already reached 32.4 percent.

The upward trend in contributions was initially driven by health and unemployment insurance. Long-term care insurance was added in 1995. While unemployment insurance contributions declined again from 2006 onwards as a result of the employment boom, health and long-term care insurance contributions continued their unabated climb.

Currently, employees and employers, who share the contribution burden, must pay an average of 17.1 percent of gross income to the statutory health insurance (GKV). The contribution rate consists of a general rate of 14.6 percent plus an additional contribution of an average of 2.5 percent, set by the individual health insurance funds. The general contribution rate for long-term care insurance is currently 3.6 percent. Childless individuals pay a surcharge of 0.6 percent, while parents with more than one child benefit from discounts. The contribution rate for unemployment insurance is currently 2.6 percent, and for pension insurance, it is 18.6 percent.

## Real net wages are shrinking

Calculations by the Leibniz Institute for Economic Research Halle (IWH) illustrate the extent to which contributions reduce employees' purchasing power, using the most recent collective bargaining agreement in the public sector as an example. This agreement provides for a 3.0 percent wage increase for federal and municipal employees as of April this year, with a minimum increase of €110 per month. Wages will be raised again in May 2026, this time by 2.8 percent.

Taking into account special adjustments under collective bargaining agreements and the elimination of the inflation bonus, the gross salary of an employee in the middle service will rise by 3.2 percent this year compared to the previous year, according to calculations by IWH economists. The increase is projected to be 3.1 percent in 2026.

Because the additional contributions to statutory health insurance and the contribution rate to long-term care insurance rose sharply at the beginning of this year, only 1.9 percent of the gross wage increase of 3.2 percent remains as net income.

After accounting for inflation – which the IWH economists estimate at slightly more than 2 percent for this year and next – the net collectively agreed wage of a mid-level employee will shrink by 0.4 percent in real terms this year. Next year, the decrease will be 0.1 percent. Although gross wages in the federal and municipal public sector have risen significantly faster than consumer prices, the IWH economists conclude that, due to rising social security contributions, «nothing will remain» after taxes.

## Brake on private consumption

The situation is similar in the private sector. In many industries, negotiated wages are rising less sharply than in the public sector due to the crisis. For example, in their latest spring report, economic research institutes are forecasting a meager increase in net wages across the economy this year of only 1.3 percent. This is insufficient to offset the rise in consumer prices of just over 2 percent.

Even disposable incomes, which include government transfer payments, barely increase after accounting for inflation. This year and next, the institute's economists expect only a meager real increase of 0.2 percent and 0.6 percent, respectively. Therefore, it is hardly surprising that consumption, which accounts for roughly 75 percent of economic output, is failing to gain momentum.

Without sweeping reforms to the social security systems, the gap between gross and net wages is likely to widen further in the coming years. This is because the aging population and advances in medical technology are driving up expenditures in pension, health, and long-term care insurance.

## Contribution rates soon to be 50 percent

A scenario analysis by the Iges Institute, an analysis and consulting firm specializing in the health and social insurance sectors, illustrates the potential consequences. In the baseline scenario without reforms, Iges projects that the contribution rate for health insurance will climb to 20 percent by 2035. For long-term care insurance, it will rise to 4.5 percent, and for pension insurance to 21.2 percent.

If the CDU/CSU and SPD implement their pension plans (mothers' pension and fixing the pension level) as agreed in the coalition agreement , the contribution rate for pension insurance will even climb to 22.1 percent, predict the Iges experts. For unemployment insurance, they anticipate an increase in the contribution rate to 3.1 percent.

If the demographic trend and the development of income and expenditure continue, «the total burden of social security contributions will amount to almost 49 percent in ten years,» says Martin Albrecht, Managing Director and Head of Health Policy at Iges. In a worst-case scenario, the total contribution could even rise to 53 percent.

## The pressure for reforms is growing

Under these conditions, it is unlikely to be worthwhile for companies and employees to create or accept jobs in Germany.

Pressure is mounting on the federal government to subject the social security system to a comprehensive overhaul. But instead of quickly setting a new course, the CDU/CSU and SPD coalition plans to first task a commission with developing reform proposals. While there seems to be a growing realization within the government that faster reforms are needed, Health Minister Warken says the commission is not bound to submit proposals only in 2027. «We will tackle things sooner,» she promises.

But how far this promise will go remains to be seen. Government members like Family Minister Karin Prien are still calling for more, not less, social spending. Prien recently pushed ahead with a proposal to introduce a care allowance as wage replacement for family caregivers. She did not specify how this would be financed.

## Longer working hours are possible

Over the past few years , countless studies by scientists have detailed what needs to be done to prepare the social security system for demographic challenges. Instead of expanding the mothers' pension and fixing the pension level in relation to earned income, they recommend raising the retirement age in line with increasing life expectancy.

A recent study by the German Institute for Economic Research (DIW) , the Ifo Institute, and Stanford University shows that older employees can also hold their own in the workplace. Based on a representative sample of adults in Germany, the researchers concluded that basic cognitive skills such as reading, writing, and arithmetic reach their peak for most people between the ages of 40 and 45.

If these skills continue to be used intensively in the workplace in subsequent years, they remain at a high level well beyond the age of 60. «Overall, the results refute the common assumption that cognitive abilities inevitably decline with age,» the researchers write. Therefore, it is crucial for individuals and society to maintain skills by using them frequently.

## Cost reduction through more market economy

While longer working lives can improve the revenue side of the welfare state, market-based reforms can counteract the expansion of spending. In healthcare, for example, a patient co-payment for treatment and medication costs, tiered according to income and severity of illness, could lead to more economical access to medical services without restricting the free choice of doctor.

Potential savings also lie in the administrative costs of social security, which, according to calculations by the Confederation of German Employers' Associations (BDA), amount to almost 25 billion euros annually. This is more than the government spends in total on parental allowance, housing allowance, and student loans, says employers' association president Rainer Dulger.

According to calculations by the BDA (Confederation of German Employers' Associations), administrative costs absorb 1.3 percentage points of the total social security contribution of almost 42 percent. If it were possible to reduce administrative costs, for example through leaner structures in self-governance and better cooperation between social security institutions, employees and employers would benefit from lower contributions. Germany needs «a social security system that operates efficiently, digitally, and cost-consciously,» says Dulger.

The federal government still lacks the courage to implement the necessary reforms. If nothing changes, the holes in the social security system and the state budget are likely to grow even larger in the future. A balanced budget – the crowning achievement of any finance minister – will then become an unattainable dream for Lars Klingbeil.
